# Washington joins the coffeehouse

*A fictional intellectual reconstruction informed by Washington’s writings and conduct. The words are original; they are not an authentic quotation or a claim that he held a later philosopher’s views.*

**Washington:** Mr. Churchill has spoken of a door that will close while its observers are still discussing the hinges. He is right to insist that the door will close. I would ask what passes through it besides the man who enters.

The novel’s most arresting discovery is that Damon’s death gives Kaelen command of an empire for forty-eight hours. The most dangerous inference is that because the command can be executed in that interval, the interval can settle who commands thereafter. Kaelen does not quite believe this. He keeps a file of those injured by his plan. He spends money to keep workers employed. He knows the calculation could be shaped by his wishes. Yet the architecture of his enterprise still suggests that once enough instructions are irreversibly sent, the chief matter has been decided. The ending corrects him. His uncle returns, finds a batch frozen, and signs it loose. The dead man’s authority still moves through the machine after the heir has taken its property. The old command has survived its owner’s dispossession.

I have been told that this table has asked too often whether Kaelen has permission to act. I will not ask it. The man who refuses to issue an order has issued one to everybody who must continue under the existing arrangement. Damon’s instructions remain in force while he studies their defects. Rosa must pay for Lianne’s medicine; Tanaka’s finding remains where he placed it; a lender writes its next contract against tomorrow’s expected fees. His forbearance has employees and beneficiaries. It is organized action. I cannot praise it as mere caution.

But I will not praise every interruption of it as liberty, either. The difference between taking a command and making others free to correct a command is the difference on which this story might turn.

I was once offered an unusually clear view of that difference. At Newburgh, the officers under my command had not received what they were owed. Their grievance was real. Congress lacked dependable funds; the states resisted providing them. An anonymous appeal urged the officers to turn their organized strength into a means of collecting the debt. I urged them against that course. An army which had fought for civil independence could obtain satisfaction by making its own judgment superior to the civil authority it had fought to establish. The injury of unpaid service would be answered by an injury of another kind. Yet after the immediate danger passed, I told a correspondent that if the debt remained unfunded, ruinous consequences could still follow. The restraint of the officers did not pay them. Their virtue could buy time; it could not become the treasury. If I had congratulated myself on having saved the republic and left the arrears to disappear into a speech about patience, I would have converted their self-command into a subsidy for the government’s failure. [The address](https://www.mountvernon.org/education/primary-source-collections/primary-source-collections/article/newburgh-address-george-washington-to-officers-of-the-army-march-15-1783) and [the letter that followed](https://www.mountvernon.org/education/primary-source-collections/primary-source-collections/article/george-washington-to-benjamin-harrison-march-19-1783) belong together.

This is why I will not accept a simple opposition between Churchill’s urgency and my supposed preference for waiting. At Newburgh I acted against men whose claim I supported. I used what influence I possessed to arrest one use of force, then pressed the public to meet the obligation that had made that use attractive. There were two clocks. On one, armed men might decide that week whether they would compel a settlement. On the other, a government unable to pay its soldiers was teaching every soldier how little its promises were worth. To stop the first clock and ignore the second would only defer a worse collision. Meadows has described the same danger in the novel’s several speeds, though she and I arrive by different roads. A reserve of time is useful only if it is spent doing the work the delay was purchased to permit.

The Bangalore meeting is therefore more disturbing to me than the red basin. Damon makes a substantial argument there. A hospital carries patients who do not recover, pays nurses before outcomes can be measured, and must keep equipment and training in place during a bad month. Kenya shows how a change that looks like justice to one worker can empty the institution that protects her next month. Damon is not inventing the ward’s needs. Then Tanaka tells him that Manila’s gap is widening. He privately holds the evidence that would let others examine Kenya more intelligently. He promises to study the matter after a different event on his calendar, and he arranges for an attestation through someone else. The machinery of postponement is as elaborate as the machinery of decision. It takes a report kept private, a slide sent to an appendix, a deputy furnished with a better annex, and a signature secured despite the person who refused it. This is not the abstinence of a man standing aside from force. The hospital’s existing terms continue to be enforced while he conducts that maneuver. To say that changing them risks harm is true. To use that truth to make his own power immune to revision is the act Kaelen has seen.

Mr. Churchill fears that my question about what follows an interruption may hand Damon a veto. I share the fear. If every preparation depends upon the incumbent’s leave, then counsel to prepare becomes counsel to surrender the opening. I would ask Kaelen to make those preparations he can make without surrendering it, and to choose when they prove insufficient. He has already done more of this than the company gives him credit for. In *Fifteen Moves* he traces the patents that finance research, the trust assets lodged in pensions, the hospital’s fixed costs, and the people likely to die between a price change and a change in supply. His estimates may be wrong. The act of making them shows that the book is not about a young man who confuses a free patent with a delivered medicine. I would not send him back to his room with an assignment to discover that markets move faster than factories. He knows. He acts.

My objection concerns the precise knowledge that could make his action corrigible. On the eleventh day in the annex, he adds *when* to a list of *what*. It is a splendid turn. Law exists in time. An officer may be named at noon and unable to enter the treasury by dusk; a reform that could pass next year may be impossible next month. The case of Gracchus gives the lesson a body: a stated rule and a man empowered to invoke it could not keep their opponents from using clubs. The Indian and English examples suggest how a moment of uncertainty may permit a new settlement. Yet Kaelen’s four conditions describe an opportunity for a person already holding an instrument. They do not tell us which people will continue to honor that instrument when the moment ends. The interval is not empty. It is full of officials doing old jobs: detecting fraud, guarding medicines, paying crews, protecting people against exactly the sort of unexplained transfer Kaelen will attempt.

The book has already placed this answer in Yangon. A clerk sees a transfer by an unexpected route and stops it. From Kaelen’s side the stoppage is a failure of his plan. From the clerk’s side it may be the faithful performance of a protective duty. Damon can release the batch because the institution still recognizes his hand. This is not proof that Damon should rule. It is evidence that an order travels along channels built before the man issuing it arrived. The path of a command deserves as much attention as the path of a fortune: who receives it, which other orders that person must obey, what makes compliance safe, and what will happen if the person refuses. That investigation can be made before the killing without being converted into an argument for waiting until the world is ready. Indeed, it might tell Kaelen where his forty-eight hours are most likely to be wasted.

I disagree with one claim the annex makes on his behalf. ARBITER tells him the conditions found among the surviving scenarios are the grammar by which a system says what it normally cannot. They may be the grammar of taking possession. They are not necessarily the grammar of founding an order under which an injured person can oppose the possessor. If a legal category has no settled name, the first person able to exploit the omission may gain a valuable power. He does not thereby gain a public argument for every exercise of it. More significantly, those who must live with the new rule may develop a different grammar of their own: the pharmacist who will dispense despite uncertainty, the scientist who will test the new carrier in humid air, the nurse who will stay when the old wage table vanishes, the mother who will refuse the surveillance clause next time. The new order is not made in a single voice. The heir’s decisive verb is followed by millions of other verbs. Some will complete his sentence; some will contradict it.

That thought brings me to the portion of my own history with which I can least comfortably sit at this table. I could surrender a military commission. I could decline another term of public office. Those acts mattered because a powerful man’s relinquishment helped make an office distinct from its occupant. But I also held human beings in bondage at Mount Vernon. I arranged in my will that those I owned outright would be freed after my wife’s death. I did not free them during my life, and the arrangement did not free all the enslaved people living on the estate. I could describe a network of obligations and legal entanglements; the people whose lives were delayed would still have lived the delay. [My will](https://www.mountvernon.org/education/primary-source-collections/primary-source-collections/article/george-washingtons-last-will-and-testament-july-9-1799) and [the estate record](https://www.mountvernon.org/george-washington/slavery/george-washingtons-will) leave that fact unhidden. I bring it up here because Damon’s best defense would be familiar to any proprietor who knows how much work depends upon his property. Dependence is real. It does not answer whether his choices created some part of the dependence or whether its cost has been charged to people denied a voice in naming it.

Nor does this confession make me a better judge of Kaelen’s act. My own failure should make my counsel less comfortable, not more authoritative. I know how readily a man who has chosen delay can make his reasons sound like stewardship. I also know how readily a man who commands disciplined force can convince himself that its first use will be the last one required. The officers at Newburgh had a genuine claim and a military means of enforcing it. Their using the means would have changed the kind of country in which the claim could be paid. Kaelen has a genuine indictment and an extraordinary instrument. His using it changes the conditions under which anyone will later be able to correct him. These analogies do not resolve his decision. They identify what his decision will put at risk besides Damon’s estate.

In the Farewell Address, I argued for a government vigorous enough to protect liberty and for the distribution of power that prevents vigor from collecting in one hand. I warned about a useful act of usurpation becoming a familiar weapon. That warning is difficult here because Kaelen’s adversary has already gathered civilizational power under private ownership. To say “obey the constituted authorities” to Rosa would be grotesque if the constituted arrangements place Lianne’s medicine behind a fee that Rosa cannot contest. But it would be equally easy for Kaelen, whose inherited credentials can reach eleven billion contracts, to regard every opposing institution as a residue of tyranny. The Yangon clerk is not simply Damon in a smaller uniform. A fraud filter that can say no to an heir might be one of the tools with which the future says no to another Damon. If Kaelen destroys it because it obstructed him once, he makes his victory more complete and its successors less free.

The first act of the new order may therefore be an act of retention. Keep the clerk able to stop an anomalous payment; change who can review the stoppage and how quickly a family can be heard. Keep the hospital’s capacity to carry a patient whose recovery produces no attractive score; change who may inspect the reserve and challenge a patent toll masquerading as its cost. Keep Ate Merced’s notebook capable of supplying medicine before a global database recovers; give its members a means of proving what is in it if a keeper becomes Bae. None of this need be completed before Kaelen opens the Estonian door. Some of it cannot be. It is the work by which the door ceases to be merely an episode of ownership and becomes a possible beginning. I would give more of it to people other than Kaelen, because his being unable to command all of them is a measure of their freedom, not a defect in his plan.

The clearest man of action in the last chapter is Damon. For three days he has reasons to withhold his signature, some born of pain and some clothed as principle. Then he signs. He does not grant his nephew a verdict of innocence. He does not recover what was taken. He meets a present claim with the power he still possesses. I would resist the neatness of saying this makes the two men the same, or that it completes their education. The signatures have different histories. Kaelen’s transfer freed millions and injured others; Damon’s released people frozen partly by Kaelen’s route and partly by an architecture Damon built to make his own override indispensable. They can both do the necessary thing and remain responsible for the conditions that made it necessary.

Mr. Churchill says that after the great decision the world asks for another, and then another. I agree. I would add that the freedom to make the next decision must be maintained while the first is carried out. Newburgh taught me that preserving civil command could not mean letting Congress evade its debt. My commission could be surrendered because other people and offices could receive it. My conduct at Mount Vernon showed how a man can celebrate public restraint while reserving private dominion for himself. Kaelen has broken one private dominion by spending an inheritance and an uncle’s life. Whether he has made room for people who can oppose *him* is not a question to put before him as a condition of entry. It is the question his entry makes urgent.

I would not end the novel by answering it from the room above the bay. The second cup remains untouched. Elsewhere, a fraud clerk still has a queue; a scientist has protocols to examine; a woman has a notebook and six families who will ask what they now owe; a nurse has a night shift. The empire has been altered, and the republic, if there is to be one, is not yet an accomplished noun. It is the work these people can undertake without waiting for either Korr to explain their lives to them.
