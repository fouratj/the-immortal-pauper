**Hume:** Locke catches me attributing too much to Damon's reputation. An admired man without the override key could not thaw Myanmar's transfers. A despised man with it might. I revise the claim: the key supplies capacity; the clerks' established practice gives its use a predictable effect. The two have grown together. The system that recognizes Damon's signature also trains operators to treat it as a safe end to a dispute. A new signature, even with the same code, would meet their justified suspicion. The final scene proves neither that opinion rules alone nor that architecture rules alone.

Hobbes makes a harder point. Six boxes of medicine cannot satisfy twelve sound claims. Trust may bring the parties to the same counter; it cannot rank their needs. Someone must judge and sometimes disappoint a person whose claim is real. I grant that necessity. I dispute that a final authority can then stand outside opinion. A judgment settles the immediate queue only if the losers expect that the rule will apply when their turn comes, that they can challenge an error, and that obeying today will not make them prey tomorrow. Force can move them once. It cannot make them plan their lives around the next judgment without becoming an occupation.

Ostrom's Bae is the objection I most needed. Three years of contributions taught eighteen women to rely on him; his theft showed that repetition can deepen exposure rather than warrant confidence. Rosa similarly knows what happened to Marco and signs CarePath anyway. I withdraw any suggestion that habitual use demonstrates approval. Dependence can look exactly like trust in a transaction log. We distinguish them when a person can object and keep her shift, seek another pool and keep her savings, refuse a contract and still buy insulin. Ostrom's appeals and Darwin's outside options are therefore part of the conditions under which a convention earns authority, not decorations to add after it has lasted.

My one digression would be called **“What a Signature Buys.”** Its home is *The Confrontation*, at Damon's pause before he signs for Myanmar. On the page already, Kaelen's satellite route triggers fraud filters, fourteen million people enter legal limbo, Damon signs, and some transfers resume while others remain tangled. Add a chain of questions within that pause. Why can a signature move a balance? Because a bank recognizes the signer. Why does the bank's recognition matter to a family in Yangon? Because a merchant expects the bank to settle, and the merchant's supplier expects settlement in turn. Why does that expectation survive mistakes? Because fraud clerks sometimes stop a payment and can later explain why they released it. Who can overrule them? At present, the man whose empire is being broken. Who will perform that role after he is gone, and why will anyone accept an adverse ruling from the replacement?

The chain can move from Damon's finger on the pad to a household waiting for medicine without becoming a tour of offices. Each answer creates the next dependency; each dependency makes an abuse possible. Damon must see that his signature is both a rescue and proof that he made himself indispensable. Kaelen must see that deleting his uncle's name from the ledger cannot instantly replace the practiced judgments beneath it. The final unresolved transfers give the explanation a cost that neither man can rhetorically erase.

The seductive claim to resist is that a system's continued use proves people trust it. Bae, CarePath, and Eduardo's legally required stamp all persist because refusing them can cost more than accepting them. The novel is missing an account of how a civilization transfers practical confidence to a new order while giving people a safe way to withhold it.
