**Hobbes:** A ledger can tell you that two people have claims. It cannot decide what happens when both are sound and medicine spoils before either yields.

Locke, you asked what power would let Carlos refuse Javi without losing care. That is the right challenge to my first speech. Enforcement of the CarePath contract would secure the investor while making the child less secure. I would not call that peace. A covenant made under a threat created by the very system enforcing it lacks the protection for which people accept authority. Yet the answer cannot be merely to pronounce the contract void. Who supplies Lianne's insulin tomorrow? Who bears Javi's bridge loan, drawn against a service he actually provided, while MentorMaxx's surveillance rights are destroyed? The obligations collide. If the patient, worker, lender, and child each veto the loss assigned to them, the insulin does not wait for their agreement. Someone must judge a priority that will injure a party with a real claim. The question is how that judgment can be effective without becoming hereditary power.

Ostrom, your appeal across scales is promising until two scales disagree. Ate Merced's seventeen families may govern their pool admirably. Suppose a hospital has six scarce boxes of patches and her circle offers cash for all six, while six unaffiliated families stand in line. Her members say she is keeping faith; the outsiders say she is hoarding. Which appeal body can order her to yield stock, and who will enforce its order on the street? You have described how each group corrects its keeper. I ask who settles a conflict between groups before their different remedies become a fight. This is not a preference for one central owner. It is the problem that brings a final authority into being even where nobody desired one.

Consider the sequence in *The Discontinuity*. At first, energy is allocated: Kaelen spends power that public researchers cannot command. Energy buys computation. Computation draws a map of future property. Property gives him the means to alter eleven billion contracts. Altering them changes prices, wages, and the solvency of institutions. Those institutions owe incompatible things to patients, staff, savers, and owners. When the old settlement fails, each points to a different rule that was valid yesterday. To act, one rule must outrank another for a time. Whoever can make that ranking stick is sovereign in the practical sense, whether named a king, a court, an algorithm, or an heir with Section 47.3 credentials. The more useful Damon's network becomes, the harder it is to imagine a life outside its rankings. His immortality threatens permanence because it keeps the same judgment at the top of each collision, not simply because he keeps his wealth.

Darwin, this is why I resist calling death the lost correction mechanism. A dead founder can be followed by another who owns the same switch. The last scene shows a different possibility and its danger: Damon, legally dead but physically restored, signs to release Myanmar's frozen transfers. He does so against his own interest because fourteen million people have become hostages to a dispute between authorities. The signature is effective; it does not legitimate the original seizure or prove his moral approval. Nor does it make Kaelen's killing necessary in every possible world. Separate these claims before the model's narrow blue path persuades us they are one.

My proposed digression belongs where Kaelen moves from Gracchus's murder to the legal opening. Let ARBITER map not another 247 futures but a chain of rival commands: a probate court names the heir; a BioGenesis officer promises Damon restoration; a Bangalore operator sees a credentialed transfer; a Yangon fraud clerk sees a stolen route. Each obeys a rule. Their obedience produces a civilizational failure because their rules cannot rank one another fast enough. Kaelen might ask which officer can safely say no, who backs that refusal, and how a decision made under emergency authority becomes contestable once the patient survives. That would make his choice less like a magic key and more like a gamble with the means of peace.

Hume, you are right that no order can command instant trust. But trust alone cannot choose between two trustworthy people with opposing duties. The book's most frightening question is not whether people will obey a wicked command. It is what happens when reasonable people obey their own commands, and those commands cannot all be fulfilled.
