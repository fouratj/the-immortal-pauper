# Chapter 4: The Bangalore Meeting
*March 3, 2049 - Korr Foundation, San Francisco*

---

The conference room on the forty-seventh floor had been designed to make visitors feel small. Floor-to-ceiling glass so clean it read as absence. A table of polished obsidian. Twelve empty chairs around a man who needed no entourage. The climate control held 19°C. The air smelled like nothing, which was its own kind of expense.

The display read HUB READINESS REVIEW: BANGALORE SOUTHERN ROUTING NODE, and beneath that, in the smaller type reserved for things that mattered more, PRE-ANNOUNCEMENT ATTESTATION. Thirteen months out, the Foundation was doing what foundations do before a large public gesture, which is make sure nothing embarrassing is plugged into it. Bangalore routed attribution for three hundred and forty million workers across South and Southeast Asia. Before the announcement, someone independent had to certify that it was measuring them correctly. The someone was Dr. Yuki Tanaka, whose audit group had a reputation for not being bought—a reputation the Foundation found useful right up until the moment it didn't.

Damon Korr sat at the head of the table. Tanaka stood at the display, hands clasped. In the corner, tablet open, Kaelen watched—theoretically taking estate planning notes.

"Walk me through it," Damon said. "The edge cases."

He asked it at every review, the way a pilot kicks the tires. Tanaka walked him through them. Latency within tolerance. Throughput headroom through 2052. Attribution accuracy at Tallinn grade in thirty-one of thirty-eight corridors, within a rounding error of it in four more. Six minutes, delivered with the brisk competence of someone who had rehearsed the boring parts in order to leave time for the other one.

"Three corridors out of tolerance," Damon said. "Appendix?"

"That was the guidance I received." She didn't advance the slide. "I'm not signing with it in the appendix."

Kaelen looked up.

Tanaka pulled up the cascade tree. "The backpropagation over-credits intermediary nodes in fragmented infrastructure. When institutional layers are dense, the algorithm attributes value to the intermediation rather than the labor. That part is known. What isn't in the literature is the slope. In Manila the gap was eleven points in 2045. It's nineteen now. This isn't an error the system is converging away from. It's an error the system is converging *toward*."

"Example."

"Let's take a care worker in Manila. A nurse named Rosa Santos." A face: late thirties, exhaustion around the eyes, scrubs washed too many times appeared on the screen. "I interviewed her. Three weeks, last November. Thirty-seven interviews across eleven barangays. Rosa was the fourth."

"The II calculates her uplift contribution at roughly forty thousand pesos monthly. She receives a fixed wage: 9,000. The hospital keeps her attribution and pays it upstream. Equipment licensing. Training certification. Overhead. She's not an outlier. She's the median of the thirty-seven."

That was all she said about Rosa. The rest—the kitchen, the adobo going cold on the stove, what Rosa had said when asked about her children—was in the interview transcripts, where it belonged. Tanaka had watched advocates put a face on a slide and let it do the arguing, and had watched rooms like this one learn to discount the face and the argument together. She wanted the number to carry the weight on its own. It could.

"You have something," Damon said. It wasn't a question. "People who won't sign always have something."

She did. It had been sitting in a folder for four months, christened by a graduate student with a taste for grandeur after the hub it would ship through. "Three parameter adjustments. Individual attribution caps from 5% to 15%. Higher inference weight for non-institutional contributions. Employer capture capped at 70%. My team calls it the Bangalore Patch. It modeled worker salaries increases of up to 35 to 40 percent." Then, before he could open his mouth: "And before you say Nairobi. Kenya capped capture at 51. We hold it at 70, deliberately. The institutions keep most of their margin. We ran Kenya forty thousand times. This is the version that doesn't do that."

"Of course you did," Damon said.

He stood. Walked to the window. Stayed there longer than felt comfortable.

"Have you ever been to Shropshire?"

Tanaka blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"There's a village called Coalbrookdale. I went in 2038. Off-site. The board wanted Barcelona. I wanted to see where it started." He turned. Almost fond. "A man named Derek runs the museum gift shop. Nine generations in that valley. He took me to St. Mary's. In the churchyard the headstones get very small and very close together. Children's graves. 'Thomas Darby, aged 8. Mary Ellis, aged 6. Two Simmons, twins, aged 5 years 3 months.' The mortality rate for foundry workers under ten was about forty percent. Fourteen-hour shifts. Coal dust. Heat that blistered skin from ten feet."

Tanaka's jaw tightened. "Mr. Korr—"

"Stay with me. Before 1709, if you wanted to smelt iron you needed charcoal. Charcoal comes from trees. Trees grow slowly. Britain was running out of forests. Then Abraham Darby figured out coke. Coal is geology. He unlocked a resource that was already there. By 1750 Coalbrookdale was the Silicon Valley of its age. And the children." He nodded at Tanaka's unspoken line. "Derek's ancestor William started at seven. Lost three fingers to a gear by twelve. Worked another thirty-four years. Lived to forty-six, which was considered a good run. There's a sketch from 1803. He's holding a hammer. You can see the missing fingers. He's smiling."

Silence.

"Reformers came. Quakers, a lot of them, which was awkward, because the Darbys were Quakers too. Same meeting house on Sundays. The reformers brought testimony: children's hands, children's lungs. The owners brought ledgers. And the ledgers said that if you took the children out of the foundry, the foundry closed, and Britain went back to buying its iron from Sweden." He looked at Tanaka. "And the ledgers were right. Not morally. Economically. The margins were that thin. Those dead children were what made the edge survivable."

Kaelen's pen stopped. *Our industrial age was built on the backs of innocent children.*

"That's a historical observation," Tanaka said. "Not an argument. Are you arguing that Rosa Santos should keep earning a quarter of her value because two hundred years ago children died in English foundries?"

"No."

"Then what are you arguing?"

Damon looked at Kaelen.

Kaelen set down his tablet. "The development ladder. Brutal early industrialization. Capital accumulation. Productivity gains. Rising wages. Then reform. Britain, America, Germany, Japan, Korea, China. The Factory Acts came after Britain had industrialized. We didn't ban child labor and get rich. We got rich enough that child labor became obsolete, and then we wrote it down."

"And the implication?"

"That Manila, Dhaka, Lagos are climbing the same ladder. That what Dr. Tanaka calls extraction is the mechanism of development."

Damon's face went flat. "That's the strawman. The timing of reform matters. Interventions that look equivalent have different effects depending on where you are on the curve. And we're bad at knowing when is when. The Coalbrookdale reformers saw extraction. They were right. They couldn't model what the extracted value was *doing*. If they'd forced wages in 1750, the foundries close. We have one sample path through history. We can't run the counterfactual. What we *can* do is look at cases where reformers changed the parameters before the curve was ready. And count the bodies."

He pulled up Kenya, 2047. Not the dashboard everyone had seen. Something denser.

Clinics blinking out across Nairobi. Then regional centers. Then Kenyatta National, Nairobi Hospital, Aga Khan—green to yellow to the gray that meant *ceased operations*. Care workers' income dropping, stabilizing, dropping. Formal employment dissolving into gig static.

Tanaka watched it with the face of a professional who has seen these curves before. Then with a different face, as the curves began to diverge from the ones she'd seen.

"Kenya's post-mortem. I commissioned it. Privately." He let the timeline accumulate. "Everyone reads Kenya as a capture story. Fifty-one percent was too low, the institutions starved. That's the public reading, and it's the one you designed around. It's wrong. The collapse starts in month two. The capture cap doesn't bind until month five." A single curve, isolated in red. "It's the individual caps. Above roughly twelve percent, a high-uplift worker's attribution becomes portable. It follows her out the door. So the best nurses go independent, and what's left inside the hospital is the patients who don't recover and the staff who don't generate uplift. The good risks leave the pool. Every insurer since Lloyd's coffee house knows what happens next."

"Adverse selection," Tanaka said, very quietly.

"Forty-three percent of healthcare institutions dissolved or restructured by month eight. They thought they were fixing a bug. They were breaking an emergent property." He didn't look at her slide. He didn't need to. "Your caps are fifteen."

Tanaka's face: someone watching a number she had chosen carefully turn out to sit on the wrong side of a line she'd had no way to see.

Worker incomes: "liberated" and down thirty-two percent. Volatility like cardiac arrhythmia. Stable wages had become equity in outcomes. Recover, earn. Die, earn nothing.

"The reformers saw extraction. They didn't model what institutions were doing with it. Risk aggregation. Insurance. Stable wages during uncertainty. Liability when treatments failed. Training. Maintenance. The margin that looked like rent was—"

"The foundation," Kaelen said.

Pride flickered across Damon's face. "Yes." A face on screen: Wanjiku Kimani, home health aide. Younger than Rosa. The photograph from before. "Old system: 8,400 shillings, stable. Reformed: average 6,100. Some months 11,000. Some months 2,400. Second job. Third. Exhaustion. Patient outcomes declined. Attribution dropped. She left healthcare. Folds clothes in Westlands now. She told the post-mortem team: 'I believed them. They had data. They seemed so certain.'"

A pause. "Mao had data too. Nineteen fifty-eight, the Plan says China will out-smelt Britain in fifteen years, so ninety million peasants feed their cooking pots and door hinges into backyard furnaces. The provincial reports were magnificent. The iron was slag. Then thirty million people starved while the numbers went up." He almost smiled. "Every movement that ever promised the worker the full value of her labor has had the same design flaw. The people who suffer are never the people who decide."

Tanaka noted, with a detachment she would not manage for long, that he had done precisely what she'd declined to. A face. A quote. A home health aide folding clothes in Westlands, deployed like a closing argument. He did it well. It worked on her anyway, which was the worst part.

Tanaka's knuckles were white on the podium. "Then the caps come down to eleven. I can rerun it tonight."

"And at eleven your salary gain is what—twelve percent? Nine? And the next threshold is somewhere you haven't looked, because you didn't know to look for this one." He let that sit. "Kenya was an incomplete solution. Mao was a complete one. Both churchyards have the small headstones."

Kaelen stood. His body had decided first. "You told her to put Manila in the appendix."

Damon turned.

"That was the guidance. The corridors that don't fit go in the back, and the headline says Tallinn grade." Kaelen kept his voice level, which took effort. "You just spent two minutes on Mao's provincial reports. Magnificent numbers on top, slag underneath. That's what an appendix is."

Tanaka looked at Kaelen for the first time since the meeting began.

"Different thing," Damon said. "Mao falsified. Nobody is falsifying Manila."

"No. You're just filing it where the reader stops." Kaelen didn't wait. "And the post-mortem. Kenya found the twelve percent line. Fourteen million people paid for that number. It's the most expensive fact in attribution economics, and it's sitting with a firm that doesn't publish. Dr. Tanaka ran Kenya forty thousand times on the wrong data because you own the right data. Somebody in Jakarta or Lima is designing the next Kenya right now, without it. By your own arithmetic, that report is filling a churchyard."

"Publish the threshold and every intermediary lobby on earth learns the exact number to fight at. Every reform in the world stops at eleven point nine. You'd be handing the incumbents a map of the minefield."

"Then give it to the auditors under seal. Give it to her." A gesture at Tanaka. "But that's not the point. The point is you've drawn one axis. Complete or incomplete. Every point on it ends in a churchyard, so the only safe place to stand is still." He picked up the stylus from the table and drew over the Kenya timeline. Two lines, crossing. "Wrong axis. The question isn't how much you change. It's whether you can take it back, and how fast you find out you need to. Kenya: partial, irreversible, and blind until month five, when the damage started in month two. Mao: total, irreversible, and the feedback was lies. Coalbrookdale: no feedback at all. The children didn't get a vote." He tapped the empty quadrant. "Nobody's tried this corner. One corridor. Caps at eleven, under your line. A kill switch. And you watch the one indicator Kenya taught you to watch: high-uplift workers leaving weekly, not at month eight. You know what to look for now. That's what Kenya bought. It's obscene not to spend it."

Damon studied the quadrant for a long moment. He had the decency to treat it as a serious argument, which was its own kind of warning.

"Contamination," he said. "You pilot in one corridor and every nurse in the next corridor hears that attribution is going portable. The good ones start positioning. Side contracts, independent registrations, a cousin's clinic. Your control group spoils in weeks. Worse: the rumor does to the neighboring hospitals exactly what the patch would have. Your pilot causes the run it was designed to detect." He took the stylus back. "Attribution isn't a drug trial, Kael. The patients talk to each other. You want to change the parameters for three hundred and forty million people, take it through governance."

"So it can't be published," Kaelen said. "It can't be piloted. It can't be done partially, and it can't be done completely. That's not caution. That's an explanation that fits every possible outcome. There is no number Manila could ever produce that would count as evidence you should move."

Silence.

"And 'take it through governance'? You have unilateral power. The kill codes still work. I've seen the logs. You could switch on one corridor tomorrow and switch it off the day after. You won't. When you say take it through governance, you're describing a choice."

Damon's face did something complicated.

"And if the rumor outruns the kill switch?"

"Then we learn from that. You didn't get the II's core algorithms right the first time. You iterated. When it comes to Rosa—when it comes to the system you already control—suddenly the only acceptable approach is infinite patience." He let the word land. "Patience is cheaper for some people than others. Depending on their planning horizons."

The temperature dropped. Damon's expression went flat—not composure. Reassessment. A glance, almost involuntary, toward Tanaka.

"That's an interesting framing," he said, "from someone whose planning horizon is entirely dependent on decisions I haven't made yet."

A warning, dressed as observation.

Kaelen held. "Rosa's timeline isn't yours."

Damon turned back to the display. "Derek. We had dinner. I asked if he resented the Darbys. He said: 'My great-great-grandfather lost three fingers so his grandson could become a schoolteacher. The suffering was real. But it's also why I'm here. Those aren't separate things.' That's the paradox. The intervention that ends the suffering might also end the pathway. That's why we build institutions that can learn."

"And the slope?" Tanaka said. "Eleven points in 2045. Nineteen now. Waiting isn't the neutral option, Mr. Korr. Every year the gap widens, the correction that eventually closes it gets larger. Closer to Kenya-sized. You're not keeping anyone away from your cliff. You're walking the next reform toward it."

"That's the cost. I'm not pretending there isn't one. I've decided it's lower than the alternative. A hundred and forty million Wanjikus, if you're wrong at Kenya's rate." He pulled up the two faces, side by side. "Put Manila in the appendix as an open research question. Sign the attestation. After the announcement, I'll fund the study myself. Study the Nairobi failure modes. Design something that preserves risk aggregation while reducing extraction. St. Mary's churchyard, Dr. Tanaka. Smaller headstones. I don't want Rosa to become the next Wanjiku. Do you?"

Tanaka gathered her materials with the precision of someone refusing to show emotion. Her finger hovered over Rosa and Wanjiku, then dismissed them. "Thank you for your time, Mr. Korr."

The attestation stayed on the display after she'd gone, signature field empty. The door had closed with a click that sounded like a verdict, though it was not yet clear whose.

---

Damon didn't look at Kaelen. He looked at the display, where the two faces still sat above the empty signature field, and beneath them, in Kaelen's hand, the crossed axes and the tapped corner.

He reached for it, and Kaelen expected him to wipe the board. Instead he saved it. Two taps, a filename too small to read from across the room. Then he wiped it.

"Her deputy is solid," Damon said. "Less attached to Manila. Make sure he has the Kenya annex before the attestation goes back out. The sealed one." He was already reaching for his jacket. "You were right about that part. She should have had it."

It took Kaelen a moment to understand what he'd been handed. Not a concession. An errand. The attestation would get signed; it would simply be signed by someone who had read the correct appendix. The argument hadn't been refuted. It had been absorbed, the way the system absorbed everything, and converted into a better-informed signature.

"You still haven't said why you won't use the codes."

"The codes work because I've never used them." Damon shrugged into the jacket. "Every regulator and every government that tolerates them tolerates them as a thing that exists and doesn't happen. The first time it happens, it becomes a thing that happens."

He stopped at the door, and Kaelen recognized the stop. It was the one that preceded a history lesson.

"Eighteen forty-two. Peel needs money, so he brings back the income tax. Sevenpence in the pound—call it three percent. Only on incomes over a hundred and fifty pounds, which meant almost nobody who wasn't a gentleman. Temporary. Three years." He almost smiled. "It's still temporary. Legally it expires every year, and every spring Parliament passes an act to bring it back. Two hundred and seven springs in a row. The previous time they'd tried it, to pay for Napoleon, they abolished it after Waterloo and ordered every record of it destroyed, so that nobody could ever do it again. Twenty-six years later, somebody did. The Americans managed it faster: one percent in 1913, a top rate of seventy-seven by 1918." He buttoned the jacket. "Emergency measures don't expire, Kael. They acquire constituencies. After the first time, the only interesting question is who holds them next."

He glanced at Kaelen on the way out—brief, assessing, the look a man gives the name in his will—and left.

Kaelen took the stairs. Three floors down he stopped on a landing, because his hands had started doing something he didn't want an elevator camera to see.

He ran the contamination argument again, and on the third pass it came apart in his hands. Expectation wasn't exit. A nurse in the next corridor could hear any rumor she liked; until the caps changed where she worked, her attribution stayed exactly where it was, and a side contract without portability was just a second job. There was no run without a door to run through. And the design problem had been solved decades ago. The cash-transfer trials in Kenya—Kenya, of all places—had varied the saturation village by village precisely so that spillovers could be measured instead of feared. The French had a phrase for the argument you finally assemble on the stairs after leaving the room. Kaelen had never appreciated how literal it was.

He had the rebuttal half-composed as a message before he felt the other thing underneath it.

Nurses couldn't run. Money could. A lender didn't need portability to exist; it only needed portability to be credible. The day a Korr pilot went live in one corridor, every hospital bond in the region would reprice. Every insurer would rewrite its terms. Every credit line to every clinic from Chennai to Cebu would tighten, and the institutions would start failing before a single nurse had moved. The risk pools wouldn't drain from the bottom, through the staff. They'd drain from the top, through the balance sheet.

Damon hadn't said any of that. Whether he'd handed Kaelen the weaker version of his own argument on purpose—the one Kaelen could break, while keeping the one he couldn't—was a question Kaelen found he didn't want answered.

He deleted the message. He didn't know his uncle was wrong. He had an argument, which was not the same thing, and he could feel exactly how it ran. The balance sheet ruled out slow. Tanaka's slope ruled out never: eleven points, nineteen, and whoever finally closed the gap would have to jump further, probably someone in Jakarta or Lima working without the annex. Which left fast, and total, and irreversible. He'd drawn that quadrant himself, twenty minutes ago, and put Mao in it.

Damon hadn't refuted the empty corner. He'd saved it to a file, and the file was now the only place it existed.

Underneath the framework: a choice wearing a constraint's clothes.

His phone buzzed. Damon: *Thursday, 7pm. Don't forget.*

---

**One week later. Pacific Heights.**

Seven days trying to prove his uncle right. Tanaka had shown one city where the gap was widening. If that was implementation failure—a Manila problem—there should be a pattern: policy gaps, deployment errors, fixable parameters. Manila, São Paulo, Lagos, Cairo. He found a pattern. The wrong one. The algorithm worked differently in late-adopter economies, and the gap was widening. Three notebooks. Same question circled: *Why is extraction getting worse instead of better?*

Just the two of them in the study where they'd played chess for fifteen years, the fire dying because neither would break eye contact to add wood.

"Why did you really dismiss Tanaka's proposal? Not the public answer."

Damon set down the Macallan 25. "Sit down. Let me show you something."

A hospital hiring a nurse in São Paulo. Denser than Manila. "In Tallinn this transaction has three nodes: nurse, hospital, payment. In São Paulo, eleven. Lagos: twelve. Cairo: ten. Same job. The algorithm isn't different. The topology is. Low-trust societies build machinery to de-risk every transaction. Notaries. Certifiers. Aggregators. Each one making trust unnecessary. In Tallinn you hire with a handshake. In São Paulo, skip three notarized stamps and you're in labor court for eighteen months. The notary is load-bearing. The friction is infrastructure."

"Because Estonian institutions are better."

"Because Estonian *culture* is different." The teaching voice. "I visited Tallinn in 2037. Everyone thinks e-residency is a technology story. That's wrong. 1.3 million people. Fifty years of Soviet occupation. When they got independence they had a cultural memory of what happens when someone else controls your records. They built systems you could audit. The digital ID was a response to trauma, not a tech demo. Brazil is a different trauma. Portuguese administration designed to extract. Three centuries of that and the culture adapted: don't trust the system. I met Eduardo in Copacabana. Stamps forty contracts a day. Four minutes each. 400,000 reais a year for ink on paper. He's not a parasite. He's a cultural fossil. His clients' grandparents got burned, and their grandparents were right."

"The II rewards that friction."

"The II attributes value to enablement. In São Paulo the intermediaries *do* enable. Nurse: 23%. Hospital: 18%. Fifty-nine percent to the verification stack. That's the cost of trust in a society that can't yet afford to trust without stamps. You can't engineer trust, Kael. Fix the culture. Strengthen courts. Educate three generations. Flatten the graph organically. Then the II works."

"How long?"

"Generations. Which is the point." He reached for the Macallan again. "In 1427 Florence taxed every household in the city and, being Florentine, kept the rolls. Back in the teens two economists at the Bank of Italy matched those surnames against the 2011 tax returns. The families at the top in 1427 are still near the top. Six centuries. The Black Death, the Medici, Napoleon, Mussolini, the euro. Culture moves at the speed of surnames, Kael. You don't hurry it with a parameter."

Kaelen filed that away without answering. It had the feel of a good argument, and something about it frightened him that he couldn't yet name.

He was quiet. Then: "The II was supposed to *be* the cultural intervention. Algorithmic verification substituting for civic norms. It worked in Tallinn, Copenhagen, Singapore—places where the culture was already there. In São Paulo, Lagos, Manila, the intermediaries got there first. They captured attribution before the II could replace them. Eduardo stamps contracts because the law requires it, and the law requires it because his association lobbied for it, and they could afford to lobby because attribution revenue funded the campaign. You say fix the culture first. The algorithm is why the culture won't change. You're not reflecting the topology. You're freezing it."

Silence. The fire cracked.

Recognition flickered. Then the mask.

"It's easy to want revolution when you're twenty-seven and have a trust fund. Don't pretend you're taking the same risk they are. You're not Rosa. You're my nephew." His hand trembled. Slightly. He set the glass down. "After the announcement. Once the governance transition settles. I'll commission a full study. We'll model it properly."

Kaelen knew the shape of that promise. Tanaka. Cairo. Lagos. Acknowledge, reframe, defer.

"Okay," he said. "After the announcement."

He left an hour later. Drove through fog, streets slick, city lights bleeding. Hands shaking on the wheel. He'd seen it: the look of someone who'd heard a better explanation and chosen not to follow it. Then additional modeling. Proper study. Always after.

He pulled over on Divisadero. Sat in the dark car, fog condensing on the windows. Thirty-seven minutes. The question—*is he right or is he rationalizing?*—wasn't the question anymore. Damon knew. Some part of him understood. And he was choosing not to look. Not because he was evil. Because looking would mean seeing, and seeing would mean acting, and acting would mean dismantling forty years of work.

The new question was worse: *What do you do when persuasion doesn't work?*

When someone has the power and the information and the capacity—and won't. Not can't. Won't.

He counted his breaths. Tried to think of another path. Failed.

Then he drove home and didn't sleep.
