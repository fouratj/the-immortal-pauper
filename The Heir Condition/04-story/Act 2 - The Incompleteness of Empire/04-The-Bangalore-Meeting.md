# Chapter 4: The Bangalore Meeting
*March 3, 2049 - Korr Foundation, San Francisco*

---

The conference room on the forty-seventh floor had been designed to make visitors feel small. Floor-to-ceiling glass so clean it read as absence. A table of polished obsidian. Twelve empty chairs around a man who needed no entourage. The climate control held 19°C. The air smelled like nothing, which was its own kind of expense.

The display read HUB READINESS REVIEW: BANGALORE SOUTHERN ROUTING NODE, and beneath that, in the smaller type reserved for things that mattered more, PRE-ANNOUNCEMENT ATTESTATION. Thirteen months out, the Foundation was doing what foundations do before a large public gesture, which is make sure nothing embarrassing is plugged into it. Bangalore routed attribution for three hundred and forty million workers across South and Southeast Asia. Before the announcement, someone independent had to certify that it was measuring them correctly. The someone was Dr. Yuki Tanaka, whose audit group the Foundation funded precisely because it had a reputation for not being bought—a reputation the Foundation found useful right up until the moment it didn't.

Damon Korr sat at the head of the table. Tanaka stood at the display, hands clasped. In the corner, tablet open, Kaelen watched—theoretically taking estate planning notes. Actually documenting everything.

"Walk me through it," Damon said. "The edge cases."

He asked it at every review, the way a pilot kicks the tires. Tanaka walked him through them. Latency within tolerance. Throughput headroom through 2052. Attribution accuracy at Tallinn grade in thirty-one of thirty-eight corridors, within a rounding error of it in four more. Six minutes, delivered with the brisk competence of someone who had rehearsed the boring parts in order to leave time for the other one.

"Three corridors out of tolerance," Damon said. "Appendix?"

"That was the guidance I received." She didn't advance the slide. "I'm not signing with it in the appendix."

Kaelen looked up.

Tanaka pulled up the cascade tree. "The backpropagation over-credits intermediary nodes in fragmented infrastructure. When institutional layers are dense, the algorithm attributes value to the intermediation rather than the labor. That part is known. What isn't in the literature is the slope. In Manila the gap was eleven points in 2045. It's nineteen now. This isn't an error the system is converging away from. It's an error the system is converging *toward*."

"Example."

"Care work in Manila. A nurse named Rosa Santos." A face: late thirties, exhaustion around the eyes, scrubs washed too many times. "I interviewed her. Three weeks, last November. Thirty-seven interviews across eleven barangays. Rosa was the fourth."

She let the photograph sit.

"The II calculates her uplift contribution at roughly forty thousand pesos monthly. She receives a fixed wage: 9,000. The hospital keeps her attribution and pays it upstream. Equipment licensing. Training certification. Overhead." Tanaka's eyes went back to the photograph. "When I interviewed her she was making dinner. Vinegar and pork fat. Adobo, her mother's recipe. She kept apologizing for the mess. I asked what she wanted for her children. She said, 'I just want them to be okay.' Like wanting that much was already asking too much."

"You have something," Damon said. It wasn't a question. "People who won't sign always have something."

She did. It had been sitting in a folder for four months, christened by a graduate student with a taste for grandeur after the hub it would ship through. "Three parameter adjustments. Individual attribution caps from 5% to 15%. Higher inference weight for non-institutional contributions. Employer capture capped at 70%. My team calls it the Bangalore Patch. Modeled worker salaries up 35 to 40 percent." Then, before he could open his mouth: "And before you say Nairobi. Kenya capped capture at 51. We hold it at 70, deliberately. The institutions keep most of their margin. We modeled Kenya from every number that's public. This is the version that doesn't do that."

"Every number that's public," Damon said.

He stood. Walked to the window. Stayed there longer than felt comfortable.

"Have you ever been to Shropshire?"

Tanaka blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"There's a village called Coalbrookdale. I went in 2038. Off-site. The board wanted Barcelona. I wanted to see where it started." He turned. Almost fond. "A man named Derek runs the museum gift shop. Nine generations in that valley. He took me to St. Mary's. In the churchyard the headstones get very small and very close together. Children's graves. 'Thomas Darby, aged 8. Mary Ellis, aged 6. Two Simmons, twins, aged 5 years 3 months.' The mortality rate for foundry workers under ten was about forty percent. Fourteen-hour shifts. Coal dust. Heat that blistered skin from ten feet."

Tanaka's jaw tightened. "Mr. Korr—"

"Stay with me. Before 1709, if you wanted to smelt iron you needed charcoal. Charcoal comes from trees. Trees grow slowly. Britain was running out of forests. Then Abraham Darby figured out coke. Coal is geology. He unlocked a resource that was already there. By 1750 Coalbrookdale was the Silicon Valley of its age. And the children." He nodded at Tanaka's unspoken line. "Derek's ancestor William started at seven. Lost three fingers to a gear by twelve. Worked another thirty-four years. Lived to forty-six, which was considered a good run. There's a sketch from 1803. He's holding a hammer. You can see the missing fingers. He's smiling."

Silence.

"Every year reformers came. Quakers, many of them—same as Darby. They documented the conditions. And every year the foundry owners said: we can't afford it. If we stop using children, the foundries close. The iron doesn't get made." He looked at Tanaka. "And they were right. Not morally. Economically. The margins were that thin. Those dead children were what made the edge survivable."

Kaelen's pen stopped. *Our industrial age was built on the backs of innocent children.*

"That's a historical observation," Tanaka said. "Not an argument. Are you arguing that Rosa Santos should keep earning a quarter of her value because two hundred years ago children died in English foundries?"

"No."

"Then what are you arguing?"

Damon looked at Kaelen.

Kaelen set down his tablet. "The development ladder. Brutal early industrialization. Capital accumulation. Productivity gains. Rising wages. Then reform. Britain, America, Germany, Japan, Korea, China. The Factory Acts came after Britain had industrialized. We didn't ban child labor and get rich. We got rich enough that child labor became obsolete, and then we wrote it down."

"And the implication?"

"That Manila, Dhaka, Lagos are climbing the same ladder. That what Dr. Tanaka calls extraction is the mechanism of development."

Damon's face went flat. "That's the strawman. The timing of reform matters. Interventions that look equivalent have different effects depending on where you are on the curve. And we're bad at knowing when is when. The Coalbrookdale reformers saw extraction. They were right. They couldn't model what the extracted value was *doing*. If they'd forced wages in 1750, the foundries close. We have one sample path through history. We can't run the counterfactual. What we *can* do is look at cases where reformers changed the parameters before the curve was ready. We can count the bodies."

He pulled up Kenya, 2047. Not the dashboard everyone had seen. Something denser.

Clinics blinking out across Nairobi. Then regional centers. Then Kenyatta National, Nairobi Hospital, Aga Khan—green to yellow to the gray that meant *ceased operations*. Care workers' income dropping, stabilizing, dropping. Formal employment dissolving into gig static.

Tanaka watched it with the face of a professional who has seen these curves before. Then with a different face, as the curves began to diverge from the ones she'd seen.

"Kenya's post-mortem. I commissioned it. The kind of firm that doesn't publish." He let the timeline accumulate. "Everyone reads Kenya as a capture story. Fifty-one percent was too low, the institutions starved. That's the public reading, and it's the one you designed around. It's wrong. The collapse starts in month two. The capture cap doesn't bind until month five." A single curve, isolated in red. "It's the individual caps. Above roughly twelve percent, a high-uplift worker's attribution becomes portable. It follows her out the door. So the best nurses go independent, and what's left inside the hospital is the patients who don't recover and the staff who don't generate uplift. The good risks leave the pool. Every insurer since Lloyd's coffee house knows what happens next."

"Adverse selection," Tanaka said, very quietly.

"Forty-three percent of healthcare institutions dissolved or restructured by month eight. They thought they were fixing a bug. They were breaking an emergent property." He didn't look at her slide. He didn't need to. "Your caps are fifteen."

Tanaka's face: someone watching a number she had chosen carefully turn out to sit on the wrong side of a line she'd had no way to see.

Worker incomes: "liberated" and down thirty-two percent. Volatility like cardiac arrhythmia. Stable wages had become equity in outcomes. Recover, earn. Die, earn nothing.

"The reformers saw extraction. They didn't model what institutions were doing with it. Risk aggregation. Insurance. Stable wages during uncertainty. Liability when treatments failed. Training. Maintenance. The margin that looked like rent was—"

"Load-bearing," Kaelen said.

Pride flickered across Damon's face. "Load-bearing. Yes." A face on screen: Wanjiku Kimani, home health aide. Younger than Rosa. The photograph from before. "Old system: 8,400 shillings, stable. Reformed: average 6,100. Some months 11,000. Some months 2,400. Second job. Third. Exhaustion. Patient outcomes declined. Attribution dropped. She left healthcare. Folds clothes in Westlands now. She told the post-mortem team: 'I believed them. They had data. They seemed so certain.'"

A pause. "The people who suffer are never the people who decide."

Tanaka's knuckles were white on the podium. "Then the caps come down to eleven. I can rerun it tonight."

"And at eleven your salary gain is what—twelve percent? Nine? And the next threshold is somewhere you haven't looked, because you didn't know to look for this one." He let that sit. "Incomplete solutions to complex systems create casualties."

Kaelen stood. His body had decided first. "Nairobi failed because the reform was *partial*. They changed attribution without touching upstream costs. That's proof incomplete reform is dangerous, not that reform is. What if they'd reduced extraction *and* the costs that made extraction necessary?"

"You're describing comprehensive restructuring of late-adopter economies. Coordination across two hundred and seventeen countries. Error correction. Institutional functions we don't fully understand. Without creating the kind of centralized power that historically gets captured. You want to change the parameters for three hundred and forty million people, take it through governance."

"So build the governance."

"It's not an alternative. It's a fantasy dressed as policy."

"And 'take it through governance' isn't? You have unilateral power. The kill codes still work. I've seen the logs. One decision. Three hundred and forty million workers. You *could* implement the Bangalore Patch tomorrow. You won't. When you say take it through governance, you're describing a choice."

Damon's face did something complicated.

"Kenya failed," Kaelen said. "That's one data point. Fourteen million casualties, specific reasons: upstream costs, overhead, risk aggregation the reformers didn't model. Those are engineering failures, not laws of physics. The scientific response to a failed experiment is to refine the hypothesis. You're using Kenya as a stop sign."

"And if the next experiment fails too?"

"Then we learn from that one. You didn't get the II's core algorithms right the first time. You iterated. When it comes to Rosa—when it comes to the system you already control—suddenly the only acceptable approach is infinite patience." He let the word land. "Patience is cheaper for some people than others. Depending on their planning horizons."

The temperature dropped. Damon's expression went flat—not composure. Reassessment. A glance, almost involuntary, toward Tanaka.

"That's an interesting framing," he said, "from someone whose planning horizon is entirely dependent on decisions I haven't made yet."

A warning, dressed as observation.

Kaelen held. "Rosa's timeline isn't yours."

Damon turned back to the display. "Derek. We had dinner. I asked if he resented the Darbys. He said: 'My great-great-grandfather lost three fingers so his grandson could become a schoolteacher. The suffering was real. But it's also why I'm here. Those aren't separate things.' That's the trap. The intervention that ends the suffering might also end the pathway. We don't know. That's why we build institutions that can learn."

"And in the meantime?" Tanaka said. "Rosa earns 9,000 pesos."

"That's the cost. I'm not pretending there isn't one. I've decided it's lower than the alternative. A hundred and forty million Wanjikus, if you're wrong at Kenya's rate." He pulled up the two faces, side by side. "Put Manila in the appendix as an open research question. Sign the attestation. After the announcement, I'll fund the study myself. Study the Nairobi failure modes. Design something that preserves risk aggregation while reducing extraction. St. Mary's churchyard, Dr. Tanaka. Smaller headstones. I don't want Rosa to become the next Wanjiku. Do you?"

Tanaka gathered her materials with the precision of someone refusing to show emotion. Her finger hovered over Rosa and Wanjiku, then dismissed them. "Thank you for your time, Mr. Korr. I'll take your feedback under advisement."

The attestation stayed on the display after she'd gone, signature field empty. The door had closed with a click that sounded like a verdict, though it was not yet clear whose.

---

Damon turned. "Estate planning notes?"

"Extensive."

"You didn't answer my question," Kaelen said. "Why you won't use unilateral power. You gave me a theory. There's a simpler answer. Once you use it, the precedent exists. Maybe Vanessa. Maybe me. The norm dies the first time it's broken."

"What's the other part?"

Kaelen hesitated. "I think you're not sure you're right."

Relief crossed Damon's face. Brief. Involuntary.

"No," he said. "I'm not."

Kaelen walked out. Found a bathroom on the forty-fourth floor—far enough that his uncle wouldn't follow. Locked the door. Hands on cold porcelain.

Fluorescent light, brutal. He looked older than twenty-seven. His uncle hadn't won cleanly. Nairobi was real. The ladder was real. The risk was real. Kaelen had landed blows too—*infinite patience*, the glance at Tanaka, the moment Damon reassessed him. Underneath the framework: a choice wearing a constraint's clothes.

The face in the mirror had the Korr jawline. Same set to the eyes. What else had he inherited?

His phone buzzed. Damon: *Thursday, 7pm. Don't forget.*

---

**One week later. Pacific Heights.**

Seven days trying to prove his uncle right. Tanaka had shown one city where the gap was widening. If that was implementation failure—a Manila problem—there should be a pattern: policy gaps, deployment errors, fixable parameters. Manila, São Paulo, Lagos, Cairo. He found a pattern. The wrong one. The algorithm worked differently in late-adopter economies, and the gap was widening. Three notebooks. Same question circled: *Why is extraction getting worse instead of better?*

Just the two of them in the study where they'd played chess for fifteen years, the fire dying because neither would break eye contact to add wood.

"Why did you really dismiss Tanaka's proposal? Not the public answer."

Damon set down the Macallan 25. "Sit down. Let me show you something."

A hospital hiring a nurse in São Paulo. Denser than Manila. "In Tallinn this transaction has three nodes: nurse, hospital, payment. In São Paulo, eleven. Lagos: twelve. Cairo: ten. Same job. The algorithm isn't different. The topology is. Low-trust societies build machinery to de-risk every transaction. Notaries. Certifiers. Aggregators. Each one making trust unnecessary. In Tallinn you hire with a handshake. In São Paulo, skip three notarized stamps and you're in labor court for eighteen months. The notary is load-bearing. The friction is infrastructure."

"Because Estonian institutions are better."

"Because Estonian *culture* is different." The teaching voice. "I visited Tallinn in 2037. Everyone thinks e-residency is a technology story. That's wrong. 1.3 million people. Fifty years of Soviet occupation. When they got independence they had a cultural memory of what happens when someone else controls your records. They built systems you could audit. The digital ID was a response to trauma, not a tech demo. Brazil is a different trauma. Portuguese administration designed to extract. Three centuries of that and the culture adapted: don't trust the system. I met Eduardo in Copacabana. Stamps forty contracts a day. Four minutes each. 400,000 reais a year for ink on paper. He's not a parasite. He's a cultural fossil. His clients' grandparents got burned, and their grandparents were right."

"The II rewards that friction."

"The II attributes value to enablement. In São Paulo the intermediaries *do* enable. Nurse: 23%. Hospital: 18%. Fifty-nine percent to the verification stack. That's the cost of trust in a society that can't yet afford to trust without stamps. You can't engineer trust, Kael. Fix the culture. Strengthen courts. Educate three generations. Flatten the graph organically. Then the II works."

"How long?"

"Generations. Which is the point."

Kaelen was quiet. Then: "The II was supposed to *be* the cultural intervention. Algorithmic verification substituting for civic norms. It worked in Tallinn, Copenhagen, Singapore—places where the culture was already there. In São Paulo, Lagos, Manila, the intermediaries got there first. They captured attribution before the II could replace them. Eduardo stamps contracts because the law requires it, and the law requires it because his association lobbied for it, and they could afford to lobby because attribution revenue funded the campaign. You say fix the culture first. The algorithm is why the culture won't change. You're not reflecting the topology. You're freezing it."

Silence. The fire cracked.

Recognition flickered. Then the mask.

"It's easy to want revolution when you're twenty-seven and have a trust fund. Don't pretend you're taking the same risk they are. You're not Rosa. You're my nephew." His hand trembled. Slightly. He set the glass down. "After the announcement. Once the governance transition settles. I'll commission a full study. We'll model it properly."

Kaelen knew the shape of that promise. Tanaka. Cairo. Lagos. Acknowledge, reframe, defer.

"Okay," he said. "After the announcement."

He left an hour later. Drove through fog, streets slick, city lights bleeding. Hands shaking on the wheel. He'd seen it: the look of someone who'd heard a better explanation and chosen not to follow it. Then additional modeling. Proper study. Always after.

He pulled over on Divisadero. Sat in the dark car, fog condensing on the windows. Thirty-seven minutes. The question—*is he right or is he rationalizing?*—wasn't the question anymore. Damon knew. Some part of him understood. And he was choosing not to look. Not because he was evil. Because looking would mean seeing, and seeing would mean acting, and acting would mean dismantling forty years of work.

The new question was worse: *What do you do when persuasion doesn't work?*

When someone has the power and the information and the capacity—and won't. Not can't. Won't.

He counted his breaths. Tried to think of another path. Failed.

Then he drove home and didn't sleep.
