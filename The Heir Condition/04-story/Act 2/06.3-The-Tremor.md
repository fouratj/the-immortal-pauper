# The Tremor
*April 18-20, 2050 — Seoul*

---

**Hour 6 — Seoul National University Hospital, Bundang**

The notification arrived at 06:17 KST, the same time it arrived everywhere else, though Seo-jin didn't know that yet. She was in the middle of an intubation—elderly woman, respiratory failure, the particular gray of lungs that had been working too hard for too long—when her phone buzzed against her thigh.

She finished the intubation. Checked the tube placement. Confirmed bilateral breath sounds. Told the respiratory tech to start the ventilator settings. Then pulled out her phone.

**ATTRIBUTION METADATA UPDATE: Version 2.0**
**Individual attribution caps: 5% → 25%**
**Inference weights: 0.25x → 0.85x**
**Effective immediately across all II nodes.**

She read it twice. The numbers were familiar—she'd seen them in policy proposals, in conference presentations, in the kind of academic papers that argued for exactly these changes and were dismissed as impractical. Individual caps at 25% meant workers received five times more credit for their direct contributions. Inference weights at 0.85x meant the algorithms weighted actual human judgment—nunchi, clinical intuition, the things that lived in the body—nearly as heavily as measured outputs.

Her phone buzzed again. Then again. Then continuously—the hospital's internal messaging system flooding with questions nobody could answer.

**DR. YOON — Emergency department reimbursement rates recalculating. Please standby.**

**ALL STAFF: Budget projections suspended pending metadata reconciliation.**

**DEPARTMENT HEADS: Mandatory meeting, Conference Room 4B, 07:00.**

She went to the meeting. The hallway was wrong—the usual morning-shift calm replaced by the particular energy of people who didn't know if their world was ending or being reborn. Nurses clustered around phones. A resident was crying in the stairwell—whether from fear or exhaustion or something else, Seo-jin couldn't tell and didn't ask, because asking required stopping and stopping wasn't available.

Conference Room 4B was standing room only. Every department head, plus the chief medical officer, plus three administrators Seo-jin had never seen before who were presumably from the consortium's financial division. Dr. Kwon—the CMO, a transplant surgeon in his sixties with the particular authority of a man who'd been cutting people open since before most of his staff were born—stood at the front with a tablet he was trying not to drop.

"The attribution updates are global," he said. "Every II node. Every hospital, every clinic, every facility on the network. The reimbursement algorithms are recalculating in real-time."

"What does that mean for us?" Dr. Choi from dermatology. His voice was tight. Seo-jin noticed his hands—clenched around his own tablet, knuckles white. She knew what the metadata update meant for dermatology. The old attribution parameters had generously valued cosmetic procedures—the result of decades of political lobbying that had embedded itself into the II's foundational weighting, creating the exact incentive structure that had driven Korea's doctor allocation crisis. High reimbursement for procedures that made people look better. Low reimbursement for procedures that kept people alive.

The update inverted the weighting.

"I don't have final numbers yet," Kwon said carefully. "But preliminary projections show—" He paused. Looked at his tablet. Looked up. "Emergency medicine reimbursement rates are up approximately 40%. Pediatrics up 35%. Obstetrics up 28%. General surgery up 22%."

Silence.

"And dermatology?" Choi's voice was barely audible.

"Down approximately 55%."

The room erupted. Seo-jin stood against the back wall and felt two things simultaneously: the visceral satisfaction of seeing her specialty valued the way she'd always known it should be valued—a satisfaction so sharp it felt almost like vengeance, which horrified her—and genuine empathy for Choi, who was a decent doctor, a good clinician, who had chosen dermatology not out of greed but because the system had made it rational. The system had told him: this is where the money is, this is where the chaemyeon is, this is what a successful doctor looks like. And he'd listened. And now the system was saying something different, and his entire career was being recalculated while he stood in a conference room trying not to drop his tablet.

Chaemyeon. The word arrived in her mind with her father's voice attached to it. Her father—Dr. Yoon Tae-hyun, retired orthopedic surgeon, currently playing golf at the Anyang Country Club because retired Korean surgeons of a certain generation played golf the way they performed surgery: with precision, intensity, and an unwillingness to admit they were tired. He'd wanted her to do dermatology. Had said *응급의학? 미쳤어?*—*Emergency medicine? Are you crazy?*—with the particular disappointment of a Korean father whose daughter had chosen competence over status.

The II had made her choice viable. Had correctly attributed emergency medicine and funded it properly and turned her career from martyr's calling into professional success. But her father had never fully adjusted. Still introduced her at dinners as *우리 딸, 분당서울대병원*—*my daughter, Bundang Seoul National*—emphasizing the hospital's prestige rather than her specialty. The chaemyeon of the institution substituting for the chaemyeon he wished her specialty carried.

If the attribution update held—if emergency medicine rates stayed at +40%—her father would recalculate. Not his love. His introduction.

She went back to the ER. Patients were arriving—the cascade's human cost, translated into bodies. A man with chest pain who turned out to be having a panic attack after watching his stock portfolio disintegrate. A woman who'd fallen down stairs while rushing to check her banking app. An elderly man—not Park Jae-won, a different elderly man, a different version of the same isolation—who'd called an ambulance because the news had scared him and he had no one else to call.

The II logged each arrival. Attributed each intervention. Generated data that would feed into next month's metrics. The system was still working. The system was *always* working. That was the thing about systems—they didn't panic. They recalculated.

---

**Hour 20 — Bundang ER**

Seo-jin had been on shift for fourteen hours. She was supposed to have left at noon. She hadn't left.

The cardiac cases were spiking—not from any medical cause but from stress. Financial stress did specific things to the cardiovascular system: cortisol surged, blood pressure climbed, the heart worked harder to perfuse tissues that were preparing for a threat that wasn't physical. The body couldn't distinguish between a tiger and a collapsing pension fund. It responded to both with the same ancient machinery: fight, flight, or cardiac failure.

She'd treated nine stress-induced presentations since the cascade began. Two genuine cardiac events—both in men over sixty, both living alone, both with social connection indices below the 20th percentile. The correlation was not subtle. Isolation plus financial shock equaled cardiac vulnerability. The II's wellness algorithms should have flagged them. Maybe they had. Maybe the flags were lost in the same system-wide recalculation that was turning every number into a question mark.

Her phone buzzed. She checked it between patients—standing in the hallway outside Bay 4, her back against the wall, the particular exhaustion of emergency medicine settling into her joints like cold water.

News headlines: **GLOBAL PATENT RELEASES CONTINUE. PHARMACEUTICAL SECTOR IN FREEFALL. "THE CASCADE" — WHAT IS HAPPENING?**

Then: **Research consortium funding models under review. Grant disbursements suspended pending metadata reconciliation.**

Her cardiac treatment. Phase III trial funding. Suspended.

She stared at the notification. Three years of research. The nanoscale delivery mechanism that could reduce cardiac mortality by 60% in late-adopter countries. The science was done—the breakthrough was real, published, replicated. What remained was the clinical trial that would prove it at scale. The clinical trial that required funding. The funding that had just been suspended because the attribution metadata that determined how research grants were allocated had been rewritten six hours ago by someone she didn't know, for reasons she didn't understand, while she was intubating a woman whose lungs had given up.

She felt something cold move through her stomach. Not fear—she was too tired for fear. Something more like the recognition that the ground she'd been standing on was not, in fact, solid. Had never been solid. Had been a surface maintained by the continuous agreement of systems that were now, suddenly, disagreeing.

She called halmeoni. The phone rang three times.

"서진아." Her grandmother's voice was calm. The particular calm of someone who had survived enough that a global economic crisis registered as *weather*. "뉴스 봤어."

"할머니, 괜찮아요?" *Are you okay?*

"괜찮지. 여기 다 괜찮아. 은지가 아침에 수정과 갖다줬어." *I'm fine. Everything's fine here. Eunji brought sujeonggwa this morning.* 

Of course she had. Tuesday. Grandfather died on a Tuesday. Eunji remembered.

"서진아, 무서워?" *Seo-jin-ah, are you scared?*

Seo-jin didn't answer immediately. She was standing in a hospital hallway, her back against the wall, fourteen hours into a shift that was supposed to be eight, treating the cardiac fallout of an economic earthquake while her research funding evaporated. Was she scared?

"좀요." *A little.*

"전쟁 때도 이랬어." Her grandmother's voice softened. *It was like this during the war too.* "모든 게 무너지는 것 같았어. 정부, 군대, 은행, 다. 근데 알잖아—"

Seo-jin closed her eyes. She knew what came next. Had heard it a hundred times. Needed to hear it again.

"그래도 살았지." *But still, I lived.*

The words landed in her chest and did what they always did—not comfort exactly, but *orientation*. A compass reading. The reminder that the ground had been unstable before, and that the response to unstable ground was not paralysis but movement. One foot, then the other. One patient, then the next. One bowl of doenjang-jjigae, then another. The system was shaking. The 그래도 was not.

"할머니, 나 다시 가봐야 해요." *I have to go back.*

"알아. 서진아—" A pause. "밥 먹었어?"

She hadn't. Of course she hadn't.

"아직요."

"미쳤어. 밥 먹어. 그리고 다 구해." *You're crazy. Eat. Then save everyone.*

Seo-jin laughed—the first laugh in fourteen hours, hoarse, exhausted, the sound of a person who was not okay but who was also not going to stop. She hung up. Walked to the vending machine. Bought a triangle kimbap—the convenience store kind, rice and tuna and processed seaweed, ₩1,500 of compressed sustenance that halmeoni would have considered an insult to the concept of food.

She ate it standing in the hallway. The attribution system logged nothing. The rice was adequate. The act of eating was everything.

She went back to the ER.

---

**Hour 35 — Bundang ER**

The turbulence was settling.

Not ending—settling. The way an earthquake's aftershocks diminish: each one smaller, each one less terrifying, each one a reminder that the ground had not yet decided to be still but was trending in that direction.

Seo-jin saw it first in the reimbursement dashboard—the numbers that had been flashing RECALCULATING for twenty-nine hours were beginning to resolve into stable figures. Emergency medicine: up 43%. Higher than the preliminary projection. The algorithms had finished their recalculation and concluded, with mathematical finality, that saving lives was worth more than the old parameters had acknowledged. Her department's funding was not just stable—it was *growing*.

She saw it second in the patient flow. The stress presentations were decreasing—fewer panic attacks, fewer cardiac spikes, fewer people arriving because the world was ending. The world, it seemed, was not ending. It was doing something else. Something the patients could feel before the commentators could name.

She saw it third in an email from the research consortium, arriving at 17:42 with the bland formatting of institutional communication that carried, this time, something seismic:

**RE: Phase III Grant Application — Targeted Cardiac Repair**
**Status: APPROVED (revised parameters)**
**Note: Patent licensing revenue projections removed from funding model per commons transition. Grant funded directly through II research attribution. Budget: increased 12% from original application.**

She read it three times. The words didn't change.

The licensing model was gone. Her cardiac treatment would not be licensed. The patent would not generate revenue for the consortium. The treatment would go to commons—available to any manufacturer, any hospital, any country that could produce it.

And her research funding had *increased*. Because the attribution system, freed from the distortion of institutional rent-seeking, had looked at her work and concluded: this is worth more than you were being paid. The value that had been captured upstream—by patent lawyers, licensing negotiators, pharmaceutical intermediaries—was now being attributed to the person who'd actually done the science.

She sat down. The nurses' station chair, the one with the broken armrest that nobody had fixed because nobody had time. She sat and she looked at the email and she did the math that she'd been doing for three years, the math that had kept her awake on the flight from Manila, the math that she'd closed her laptop on Thursday morning because the numbers were wrong and she couldn't fix them.

Manufacturing cost of her cardiac treatment: $14 per dose.

Under the old licensing model: $5,400 per treatment course. Affordable in Seoul. Unaffordable in Manila.

Under commons: $14 per dose. Affordable everywhere.

$14.

She thought of the nurse in Manila. The one whose name she hadn't remembered. The question she couldn't answer: *"Why does your medicine cost three months of my salary?"*

Because of patents. Because of licensing. Because of a system that correctly attributed the value of innovation but then allowed intermediaries to capture the delivery. Because the science was global but the economics were local and the locals who needed it most were the ones who could afford it least.

$14.

The number sat on her screen like an accusation and an absolution simultaneously. Everything she'd built—three years of research, thousands of hours in the lab, the grant applications and peer reviews and conference presentations—all of it was now worth more to the world and less to the consortium. The institution that had funded her work would earn nothing from it. The world that needed her work would pay only what it cost to make.

She should feel loss. The consortium's IP portfolio was collapsing. Her colleagues in research divisions across the hospital were scrambling to understand what the commons transition meant for their careers, their funding, their futures. The old model—innovate, patent, license, profit, reinvest—was gone. The new model was: innovate, publish, commons, fund directly through attribution.

She didn't feel loss. She felt something else. Something that started in her chest and spread outward with a warmth that wasn't the ondol floor and wasn't the coffee and wasn't the exhaustion converting into something lighter.

She felt like the development ladder had reached its top and, for the first time, was reaching back down.

Not charity. Not aid. Not the trickle-down economics that Damon Korr had promised and that had trickled sideways into patent portfolios and licensing fees. Actual structural change. The elimination of the intermediary layer that had stood between her science and the people who needed it. The free market—the *actual* free market, stripped of the rent-seeking that had made it a captured market wearing a free market's clothes—doing what it was supposed to do. Connecting the person who made the thing with the person who needed the thing, at the cost of the thing, without a toll booth in between.

She thought about halmeoni's foreman Bae. The bamboo stick. The savings pool he stole. 옛날에는 사람만 믿었어—*in the old days, we only trusted people.* And when the people failed, there was nothing.

The patent system wasn't Bae. It wasn't theft. It was legitimate, legal, properly governed. But it had the same *shape* when viewed from Manila: value created in one place, captured in another, the people in between paying for the privilege. And now the shape had changed. The toll booth was gone. The value flowed directly. Trust was embedded in the infrastructure rather than mediated through intermediaries who extracted their percentage for providing it.

Her phone buzzed. A message from an unknown number—a researcher at the University of the Philippines Manila, reaching out through the academic network:

**Dr. Yoon — I've been following your cardiac delivery mechanism research. With the commons transition, we believe we can begin local manufacturing within 6 months. Would you be willing to consult on adaptation for tropical supply chain conditions? — Dr. Reyes, UP Manila Cardiology**

Reyes. A different Reyes. Or maybe related—the Philippines was like Korea that way, the same surnames recurring across generations, families connected by name even when the connections had been forgotten.

She replied: **Yes. Send me the specs. I'll review tonight.**

She didn't know that tonight she'd be on shift again, or that the specs would wait until Saturday, or that the consultation would turn into a collaboration that would span two years and result in a manufacturing facility in Quezon City that produced her cardiac treatment for $11 per dose—cheaper than her own estimate, because the Filipino engineers found efficiencies she'd missed.

She didn't know any of that yet. She just knew that the number was $14. And that $14 was the answer to the question she hadn't been able to answer in Manila.

---

**Hour 48 — Mapo-gu, Seoul**

She went home at 20:00, forty hours after the broadcast, thirty-four hours after she should have left. The ER was stable. The reimbursement rates had settled. The patients were being discharged into a world that was different from the one they'd entered.

The apartment smelled like nothing—she'd been gone too long for the doenjang-jjigae to leave its trace. The batch from Sunday was in the fridge, six days old now, past its peak. She heated it anyway. Sat at the kitchen table. The medical journals were where she'd left them. The laptop was where she'd left it. The grant application was still open to Section 4.2: Projected Licensing Revenue.

She deleted Section 4.2. Replaced it with: **4.2: Commons Distribution Model.**

The cursor blinked. The jjigae was warm. Outside, Seoul hummed—the particular density of ten million people adjusting to a world that had been restructured while they slept and worked and panicked and survived.

She called halmeoni.

"서진아. 살아있네." *Seo-jin-ah. You're alive.*

"살아있어요." *I'm alive.*

"밥 먹었어?"

"지금 먹고 있어요. 된장찌개." *I'm eating now. Doenjang-jjigae.*

"일요일 거? 벌써 6일 됐잖아." *From Sunday? That's six days old.*

"괜찮아요. 된장찌개는 오래될수록 맛있잖아요." *It's fine. Doenjang-jjigae gets better with age.*

"사람도 그래." *So do people.* A pause. "서진아. 무슨 일이야? 목소리가 달라." *Seo-jin-ah. What's going on? Your voice is different.*

Seo-jin looked at her laptop. The commons distribution model. $14 per dose. The email from Dr. Reyes in Manila.

"할머니, 기억나요? 제가 만들고 있는 심장 치료제요. 비싸서 가난한 나라에서는 못 쓴다고 했잖아요." *Halmeoni, remember? The cardiac treatment I've been developing. I told you it was too expensive for poor countries.*

"기억하지." *I remember.*

"이제 14달러예요. 누구나 살 수 있어요." *Now it's $14. Anyone can afford it.*

Silence on the line. Long enough that Seo-jin checked if the call had dropped.

"할머니?"

"14달러." Her grandmother repeated the number slowly. "진짜?"

"진짜요."

Another silence. Then, very quietly: "송기죽 생각난다." *I'm thinking about the bark porridge.*

Seo-jin didn't understand. "뭐요?"

"송기죽. 1951년에 먹던 거. 솔껍질로 끓인 죽." *The bark porridge. From 1951. The one made from pine bark.* Her grandmother's voice had changed—softer, more distant, as if she were speaking from the camp in Busan, from the winter of 1951, from the place where a seven-year-old girl ate tree bark and lived. "그때는 약이 있어도 살 수가 없었어. 돈이 없어서. 그냥 기도하고 기다리는 거였어. 누가 나으면 운이 좋은 거고, 누가 죽으면... 그래도 살았지." 

*Back then, even when medicine existed, we couldn't buy it. No money. You just prayed and waited. If someone got better, they were lucky. If someone died... but still, we lived.*

"근데 이제는—" *But now—*

"이제는 14달러." *Now it's $14.*

"네."

She heard her grandmother breathing. The sound of an eighty-nine-year-old woman processing the distance between bark porridge and fourteen-dollar cardiac treatment. Between a world where medicine existed but was unreachable and a world where medicine existed and was available. Between 1951 and 2050. Between the girl who carried her brother on her back and the granddaughter who'd built the thing that might save someone else's brother on the other side of the world.

"잘했어, 서진아." *You did well, Seo-jin-ah.*

The words were simple. In Korean, from a grandmother, they carried everything: pride, love, the accumulated weight of a life spent surviving so that this moment could exist. 잘했어. Two syllables. The attribution system had no metric for them.

"할머니. 자요. 내일 일요일이에요. 제가 갈게요." *Halmeoni. Sleep. Tomorrow is Sunday. I'll come visit.*

"약과 가져와." *Bring yakgwa.*

"성심당에서?"

"당연하지." *Obviously.*

Seo-jin hung up. Finished the jjigae. Washed the bowl by hand. Looked out the window at Seoul—the lights, the density, the city that hadn't existed when her grandmother was eating bark, the city built on the bones of a development model that had chewed up people like Soon-ok and used them as fuel and then, eventually, improbably, produced a granddaughter who built cardiac treatments for fourteen dollars.

The development ladder. The thing Damon Korr had defended in his conference room with Coalbrookdale and Derek and the children's graves. The thing Kaelen had destroyed and rebuilt in forty-eight hours. The thing halmeoni had climbed without knowing it had a name, one rung at a time, bark porridge to factory floor to jeonse apartment to care facility with heated floors and sujeonggwa on Tuesdays.

The ladder was still there. It was just shorter now. The rungs were closer together. The distance between bark porridge and fourteen-dollar medicine had compressed, and the compression wasn't charity—it was the market, actually working, value flowing to where value was created, the intermediaries stripped away, the toll booths gone.

Seo-jin closed her laptop. Set her alarm for 09:00—early enough to drive to Daejeon for yakgwa before visiting halmeoni at two.

She went to bed. Slept well for the first time in three days.

Tomorrow was Sunday. She'd bring yakgwa. Halmeoni would complain about the drive. They'd eat together in a small room with a heated floor. And Seo-jin would carry the $14 in her chest the way halmeoni carried the bark porridge—not as a number, but as a distance. The distance between what was and what could be. The distance that the system had finally, imperfectly, begun to close.

그래도 살았지.

But still, we lived.
