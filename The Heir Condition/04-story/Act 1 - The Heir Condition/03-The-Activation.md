# Chapter 3: The Activation
*April 18, 2050 - 18:12 Coordinated Global Time (11:12 AM Pacific)*

***

"I'm sorry, sir. The schedule's changed."

Kaelen stared at the security officer—different face from the one he'd mapped six weeks ago. New insignia. New problem.

"Changed how?"

"Mr. Korr is in final preparation. No visitors backstage. Family included."

Eighteen minutes, if the speech started on time. A year of work. One officer who did not know what his tablet was currently preventing.

"There must be a mistake. I'm his nephew. I'm on the access list."

"Revised this morning. São Paulo last week. Direct access requires Mr. Korr's personal authorization." A scroll. "Wasn't requested."

Six weeks ago, *family* had meant the door opened. Kaelen's left thumb pressed into his palm: the old habit, the one from childhood, the one his mother used to unfold finger by finger, saying *you don't have to hold it in*. He hadn't let it out since her funeral.

His hand found the pin. Forty-seven grams of platinum and sapphire. Seventy-seven million jailbroken nanobots in biocompatible gel, waiting for a contact that might not happen.

"Can you radio him? Tell him Kaelen needs two minutes."

The officer's face went from bored to interested. "I can't interrupt final prep—"

"He'll want to see me." He heard the desperation and hated it. "Family matter. Please."

A study of the suit, the resemblance, the sweat at the temples the climate control had not been designed to explain.

"Wait here."

The officer spoke into an earpiece. Kaelen stood in a corridor that smelled of stage fog and industrial cleaner and realized he had stopped breathing—Stanford, exams, coming up purple while classmates stared. He forced air in.

The officer returned. "Two minutes. Through there."

The preparation room had mirrors Damon had ignored. He stood by the window mouthing a speech, charcoal suit, the confidence of a man who had already beaten the thing he was about to name.

He turned. "Kael. Nervous for me?"

The smile was the real one. Not cameras. Not investors. The one that said *I see you*.

"Something like that." Kaelen crossed the room. His legs had stopped asking permission. "I wanted to—before you go out there—"

He embraced his uncle.

The pin pressed between them and shifted; the substrate found fabric. That was happening in a universe where this was a plan. In this one, Damon was holding him, and Damon smelled like sandalwood, and Damon's hand patted his back twice, the way it had since Kaelen was twelve.

Damon would be back in forty-eight hours. The backup would have the memories, the voice, the cologne. This configuration—this heart against Kaelen's chest, this particular warmth—would not. The timer in Kaelen's pocket had already started.

"Good luck," he said into the shoulder, which meant the speech and also did not. "I'm proud of you."

Damon held on the way he had at the funeral, which was the way you held family.

"Thank you, Kael. That means more than you know."

They separated. Kaelen smiled. The smile was not fake. That was the worst of it: love and murder using the same face.

Seventy-seven million units, eleven months asleep in the pin, woke to warmth and pressure and began the short, uninteresting trip from cloth to skin to blood. They would be welcomed as Gen-7s. They would close a coronary artery. The rest was plumbing.

"I should get to my seat."

"Third row, right? I'll find your face in the crowd."

*Don't.*

"I'll be there."

He left before sandalwood could turn him around.


**18:29 Coordinated Global Time**

A guard at the main entrance turned the pin over in his palm.

"Pretty fancy."

"Family heirloom."

The substrate was empty. Kaelen re-pinned it over his heart with fingers that had no right to be steady.

VIP section. Third row. Seat 17. Leather cold through wool. A woman with a silk handkerchief; a man muttering timestamps. The music swelled. The podium rose.

Damon found him, as promised, and smiled.

Kaelen smiled back.


What happened on the stage, the world has. Kaelen heard the sound a body makes when it meets polished floor—small, like a book falling—and that was enough. He sat through the screaming and the medical theater and Dr. Chen's headshake because the mask required sitting, and because the mask had fused to him and he could no longer locate the seam.

His phone began to vibrate.

He stood on someone else's legs, nodded at a face he did not see, and found the secure room he had mapped six months ago: administrative section, third floor, lock that knew his biometrics, carpet that smelled of cleaner and disuse.

He locked the door. Sat. The phone did not care that he was coming apart. Thousands of authentication requests. The cascade, queued. Forty-eight hours before a new body opened its eyes. Compute allocated through Hour 52; his gaze slid off the number the way it had for three months.

He opened the first transfer. Mis-typed the confirmation twice. Three times.

One temporary death versus a trillion lives: the clean version. The version that did not include sandalwood, or two pats on the back, or a smile that had not been a performance.

He authorized the transfer.

Then he bent over and vomited onto the carpet: bile and coffee, throat burned, room circling. He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand.

He authorized the next. And the next.

The cascade ran. Kaelen sat in a locked room that smelled of vomit and industrial fiber, crying too hard to see the screen, dismantling an empire one confirmation code at a time.

*I'm sorry, Mom. I tried.*

Another. Another.


At Hour 4, he moved.

The bunker was an hour by automated aircraft: ozone, cold recycle, the same air as the night the models had shown him a red basin and one blue line. He made coffee. Sat down. Continued.
