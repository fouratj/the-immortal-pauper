# Chapter 1: The Portfolio

Javi was doing sixty on a Honda Metropolitan through Quezon City, weaving between jeepneys in competing shades of chrome and rust, when his glasses pinged again. Carlos M. Third incident this week. Blue dot not moving.

He ran the numbers the way you ran them if you wanted to stay in this business: three incidents was a pattern. Pattern meant the models would start eating the kid's projected ₱4M lifetime uplift from the bottom. His fifteen percent of that was ₱600K on paper. Paper burned. ₱125K of other people's money. Four months. A fourteen-year-old currently ignoring his calls.

Cubao-Quiapo bus two meters left, San Miguel and faded Korean skincare on the panels. Relay nodes on every third pole, matte-white, gecko-warm. The Vizor Pro 3 laid Carlos over the world as a pulsing blue. The Axon on his shoulder was recording. After Gutierrez, everything got recorded.

April humidity: not rain, not sweat, just wet. Somewhere a lugaw cart—rice porridge, fried garlic, 2 AM if you were young. Javi wasn't young. A banner crawled the top of his display: **KORR FOUNDATION ANNOUNCES GLOBAL BROADCAST — APRIL 18 — "HUMANITY'S NEXT CHAPTER"**. He dismissed it with a blink.

The blue dot stayed put.

---

Rosa Santos clocked out at 21:52. Twelve hours. Forty-seven patients. The second code had been Mrs. Reyes in Ward C—sixty-eight, arrhythmia the night resident had called anxiety. Rosa caught it from the doorway: gray around the lips, breath doing a thing breaths did when a heart forgot the beat. She called it before the monitors did. Dr. Aquino, three months in, gave her the look they all gave: half thanks, half resentment, the *thank you* stuck behind his teeth because saying it out loud would mean he'd been about to kill someone.

She knew what dying looked like. She knew almost-dying. She knew Tagalog the same way: in the body, no translation.

Scrubs: iodine, recirculated April, AC losing. In the elevator she checked the Prism 9, cracked protector, rubber band where the clip had broken. ₱8,240. Lianne's GlucoSync: four days. ₱4,800. Paycheck Tuesday. She closed the app. She'd done the arithmetic six times already.

Three nurses got on, comparing scores. Rosa had turned hers off last week. Not shame. Refusal.

"—biggest announcement since the II rollout. My cousin does security at BioGenesis. Lockdown for months."

"Korr's been promising next chapters for twenty years."

Rosa had seen Damon Korr on a billboard from the MRT once—that unlined face diagnosing the species from altitude. She'd thought, sharp: *Ikaw, naghintay ka na ba sa elevator na amoy bleach?* She wasn't usually bitter. Bitterness cost. That face had found a nerve anyway.

The elevator dinged. She left them to it.

Marco's door was closed. Four months. Neighbor TV—*Eat Bulaga*, manic laughter through concrete. A baby down the hall. The adobo she'd left in the pot: pork fat going cold, vinegar in the humidity.

---

Javi parked thirty meters from the chain-link, squeezed between a sari-sari rack—Marlboro, Nescafé sachets hanging like prayer flags—and a tricycle whose sidecar still said GOD BLESS OUR TRIP. Open drain. Garlic. Rotting fruit from the Divisoria overflow.

Carlos on a concrete barrier with five others, mid-teens to early twenties. One smoking. Talking like people who had nowhere else to be.

Glasses painted Carlos green. The rest went gray: no ID, no feed. He checked the contract while the camera ate tape. Fifteen percent. Month 4. Eight good interventions, two failed. One more failed contact and the breach clock started.

He could not go through the fence without permission. He could not take Carlos. Advisory only.

Text: *Hey man, saw you're in Tondo. Everything good?*

Nothing.

Call. Through the fence: Carlos looked at the screen, declined.

The older one had been sitting far from Carlos. Stood. Walked to the fence. Twenty-two, twenty-three. Tank top, old scars on the left shoulder. The look you gave a cop. Or a landlord.

"Who are you?"

Javi held up the guild badge. "I'm Javi. Carlos's development partner. His mom asked me to check on him."

"Development partner." Tasting it. "That's what they're calling it now? Back in my day we just said 'equity extractor.'"

"I work with him. School, job prep. His mom and I have a contract."

"You're getting paid to follow him around."

"I get paid if he does well. Graduates. Gets a job."

"So you're betting on him?"

"Yeah."

"How much?"

"Fifteen percent of his uplift. From baseline."

The guy nodded. "*Putangina.*"

"Just to talk to him. His mom's worried."

"He's fine."

"I can see that. She can't. Late night, school night, didn't go home."

The guy turned. "Oy, Carlos. You know this guy?"

Carlos's face did something complicated. Mostly resignation.

"Yeah."

"He says your mom sent him."

"She always does, naman."

"He doesn't want to talk to you."

"Five minutes."

Axon. Vizor. Alone. More tech on his face than most families here made in a year.

"Five minutes. You try anything stupid, we throw you out."

"Fair."

The fence opened. Javi walked through.

---

Rosa at the kitchen table, plastic cloth sticky under her elbows. Cold rice. Adobo with the fat gone white. Fan overhead, one blade bent: *tick-tick-tick*.

Marco's door. Bass through plywood—"Spring Day," the same BTS track, the one from before the facility. He used to sing it in this kitchen while she cooked, badly, loudly, doing a thing on the high notes that made Lianne slide off her chair. *Ate, I'm an artist.* *You're a disaster.* Rosa at the stove thinking: this. This exact sound. Garlic, vinegar, her children laughing over a pop song.

The song was the same. The kitchen was the same. What came through the door now was just bass. Bones of a melody.

Prism 9: ₱8,240. Mercury Drug, ₱4,800 Friday. CarePath offer from yesterday. ₱145,000 upfront. GlucoSync for two years. Monitoring. Emergency. Maybe Marco's therapy—the private kind, not the government clinic where you waited three hours to talk for fifteen minutes.

She'd opened the tab fourteen times.

She clicked Review full terms.

Forty-seven pages. Section 8: Intervention Authority. Dense on purpose. Rosa was a nurse. She knew Latin that hid ugliness. *Escalated intervention* meant someone else decides. *Increased monitoring* meant watched. *Residential treatment placement* meant they take them. *Emergency medical intervention* meant they take them at night.

She knew because she'd seen it. Different company. Same clause. Three years ago.

She looked at the door. The song about missing someone.

---

"So you're the guy making money off kids."

Nineteen, on an upturned crate. The one who'd opened the fence answered first. Up close the shoulder scars weren't random. Cigarette, maybe. Eyes flat.

"I provide services. Family gets money up front. I get equity. It's a trade."

"Services. Like following him around at night."

"Tutoring. Mentorship. Job prep. Crisis. Following him is how I know when crisis is needed."

Carlos on the barrier, watching. The other four had drifted into a semicircle. Not a threat yet. Paying attention.

"You ever hear of Marco Santos?"

Javi's face did something. The guy smiled. First expression. Not friendly.

"One of yours?"

"Not mine. Different specialist."

"Same contracts."

"I don't know what happened with Marco—"

"I'll tell you, then." Police-report voice. "Guy named Ernesto. Eighteen percent. Marco started slipping. Ernesto filed breach, sold the contract to MentorMaxx for ₱60K. They showed up at 2 AM. Private security. Facility, six months."

Javi's stomach dropped.

"Marco came back. Doesn't leave the house. Doesn't talk." A pause. "So when I see you here with your equity stake, I think about Marco."

"I'm not MentorMaxx. I don't do residential—"

"What happens when Carlos stops answering? Two years in, baseline dropping?"

Javi didn't have a good answer.

"You file. Same as them. Maybe you sell. Then maybe 2 AM."

"That's not—I wouldn't—"

"Marco's mom probably said the same thing."

The nineteen-year-old: "Rico, he's just doing his job, lang."

Rico. Javi filed it.

"His job is making money off kids who don't know they're investments."

"The contract is with the mother."

"Because she was desperate. And Carlos is fourteen."

Carlos stood up. "I'm right here. Stop talking about me like I'm not."

Rico looked at him. "You want him here?"

"Doesn't matter what I want, naman. My mom signed. If I blow him off, someone worse shows up." To Javi: "We're both stuck."

---

Rosa called Luz at 22:17.

Third ring: "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong, naman. I just—can we talk?"

"...I'm dealing with something. Carlos is out late again. I sent Javi—"

"Javi... the intervention specialist?"

Pause. "How do you know about that?"

"You told me last month. When you quit the night job."

"Oh. Right." Distracted. "He just texted. Carlos is fine. He's talking to him now."

"How much did they pay you?"

"Rosa—"

"How much?"

Four seconds. "₱100,000."

"Upfront?"

"Yeah."

"And you used it for what?"

"Rent, six months. Carlos's school fees. I quit the restaurant. I'm home more."

"Is it better?"

"Yeah. Grades a little. We eat dinner. I'm not falling asleep on the train."

Rosa looked at Marco's door. "What about when it gets worse?"

"Why would it—"

"Because he's fourteen. Investors get nervous, and then—"

"Javi's not like that."

"Ernesto probably wasn't either, di ba?"

Quiet. Then: "You're thinking about signing one too?"

"Lianne needs insulin. ₱4,800 a month. I make ₱9,000."

"There has to be another way—"

"Government waitlist is eight months. Medical loans eighteen percent. The hospital won't raise me."

"What about Marco?"

Rosa laughed without humor. "He still won't come out of his room."

"I'm sorry, talaga."

"It's not your fault, naman."

"But you're thinking about doing it again. With Lianne."

Rosa didn't answer.

"Rosa. After what happened to Marco—"

"If I don't sign, she doesn't get insulin. Then I lose her too. At least this way she has a chance."

---

Rico pulled a knife.

Eight inches, held low. Not yet a threat. Present. The sari-sari bulb caught the blade.

Javi's hand went toward the panic button and stopped. Police meant everyone here got arrested or ran. Carlos got flagged. Worse.

"I'm not taking anyone. I don't have authority—"

"You've got money riding on him. That's your authority."

"I advise. If Carlos tells me to leave, I leave."

"Then leave."

Javi looked at Carlos. "You want me to leave?"

Carlos looked at Rico, then Javi, then his phone. "If you leave, you file non-responsive. Three in thirty days and my mom can terminate you."

"Yeah."

"Then you sell me. To cover your losses. Someone like MentorMaxx. Distressed contracts, forty cents on the peso. Then they extract."

"So if I tell you to leave," Carlos said, "someone worse shows up. Not because my mom chose them. Because you sold me."

Rico's hand tightened. "*Putangina*, that's fucked up."

Megawide Construction shirt, sleeves cut off, mid-twenties: "Rico, put it away."

"He's exploiting them—"

"And stabbing him makes it worse. Carlos gets arrested for accessory. Baseline goes to shit. Then MentorMaxx definitely gets him, you idiot."

Rico didn't lower it. "So we just let them turn kids into investments?" Three friends into MentorMaxx in two years. Two came back different. One didn't come back. "You know what Korr's announcement is gonna be? Life extension. They're already living to 120. Pretty soon immortal, and we'll be their portfolios forever."

"I said stabbing him doesn't fix it."

The Megawide guy looked at Javi. "Guild certified?"

"Yeah."

"Independent? How many contracts?"

"Twenty-three."

"How many sold to MentorMaxx?"

"None."

"Breaches filed?"

"Four. First year. None went to MentorMaxx—two terminated me, one I worked out, one aged out."

"What happened to the kids?"

"Two are fine. One dropped out. One's in college."

A slow nod. "Rico, give it up, pare. He's one of the better ones."

"He's still part of the system—"

"We're all part of the system."

Rico looked at Carlos. Carlos looked back.

"I'm not going with him," Carlos said. "But I'll go home. On my own. In like thirty minutes."

"That enough?" the construction guy asked.

Javi pulled up the form. "If he's home in an hour, I can log it successful."

"Then that's what happens. Put it away."

Five seconds of eye contact. Rico folded the knife.

Javi's hands were shaking. He kept them at his sides.

---

Rosa's finger hovered over Accept.

Kitchen dark. Fan dead. Cold adobo, mildew in the walls. ₱145,000. No more counting days until the nanopatch died. Marco's intake. Rent ahead. The relief, thought of as a physical thing, like sitting down.

Then Marco. The door. Bass: *I'm still here but I'm not coming out.*

The night they came. 2 AM. Unmarked polos, polite like bank tellers. Ernesto's extraction. Six months. He came back quieter. Wouldn't leave the room. Wouldn't look at her.

Thumb still on the glass.

She thought, absurdly, of the nursing school talent show. 2038. "Ang Huling El Bimbo"—Eraserheads, not the cover. Something happened to her voice on the bridge that had not happened before or since: throat opening, not technique, the song finding the shape of her body. The auditorium went listening-quiet. Two hundred people stopping thinking about themselves. For three minutes and forty-seven seconds she was not Rosa Santos, nursing student, already counting extra shifts for tuition. She was a voice in a room.

Second place. Gift certificate to National Bookstore. Anatomy flashcards.

She hadn't sung in front of anyone since. Shifts got longer. *Someday, when things settle* filled up: singing, the watercolor set, Baguio with Luz, the version of her that found out what the voice could do. A room with a closed door. After a while she stopped noticing the door.

Through the wall, the neighbor's set had gone to late news: Korr, tight-lipped, tomorrow's broadcast, humanity's next chapter.

The button waited.

Marco's door stayed closed.

Lianne's patch: matte gray hexagons on an eight-year-old's arm. Friday. Four days.

Rosa's finger trembled.

---

Javi walked back to the scooter at 22:41, legs unsteady. The sari-sari owner watched through the window—old woman in a faded daster, face lit blue by a phone, not pretending she hadn't seen.

He logged it. Location, time. Advisory provided. Beneficiary: voluntary compliance within one hour.

SUCCESSFUL.

Notes field. He started to type about Carlos understanding the terms, the knife, the family—

Deleted it. *Beneficiary complied voluntarily. No issues.*

Submit.

The needle: +7.1%. Barely moved.

Then a flag he hadn't asked for. Carlos M. Family connection: Rosa Santos. Pending CarePath, daughter Lianne, age 8. Prior: Marco Santos, 17. FAILED.

He stared. Rico's story. Rosa's son.

He did not open the public file. He already knew the shape of a cliff. Carlos's blue dot was moving. Home, like he'd promised.

He started the scooter. EDSA at midnight, still packed. Diesel, sewage, frying oil, a burst of sampaguita at an intersection.

Rico's question rode with him: *What happens if Carlos stops responding?*

---

Home at 00:23. Studio off Timog, ₱18,000 a month, lobby guard, working elevator—families researched specialists. Twelve square meters: bed, desk, hotplate, a bathroom you could touch both walls of.

Vizor on the dock. Axon dumping to the Nexus Puck, warm aluminum, status LED. Thirty-four minutes, 2.1GB. A whole night, reducible.

The cheap TCL had defaulted to news, Korr's face faintly green: **KORR GLOBAL BROADCAST — 11 HOURS, 7 MINUTES**. He switched it off.

Filed: Carlos M. / Intervention 13 / 2050-04-17.

Two redemption requests. ₱87K from one, ₱39K from the other. He had ₱34,000 liquid.

His phone buzzed: Carlos home. 00:47. Small win. He didn't have to update the form.

He opened the guild app anyway. Cross-reference: Santos. Ernesto's last note before breach: *Pattern deviation 0.91. Recommend escalated intervention or termination.* Three days: breach. Six: sold, ₱60K. Eleven: extraction, 02:14.

Javi closed it. Opened Carlos. Added: *Family history. Relationship-based. Avoid escalation.*

Saved.

Alarm 06:30. Three check-ins, investor call, guild seminar.

Rico: *So we just let them turn kids into investments?*

Carlos: *We're both stuck.*

He slept.

---

Javi had gotten the call the afternoon before, at 14:30. Conference line. Eight names. Two talking. The rest listening.

"Javi." Del—not a real name. None of them used real names. "How's Manila? Still hot?"

"Yeah. Humid."

"I keep saying I'll visit. Never do." Papers. "I've got another call in fifteen. Seven point one percent."

Javi had practiced. "The quarter isn't over. Three contracts approaching baseline correction milestones—"

"Seven point one versus eleven point two guild average. Three consecutive quarters."

"If the milestones hit, ten point eight—"

"Sure. If." Checklist voice. "How many breaches this year?"

"None."

"Zero." The papers stopped. "Gutierrez paper. Specialists who file zero underperform eight to twelve percent against two-to-four. You don't have to agree. I'm telling you what the data says. I've got a specialist in Jakarta. Fourteen point three. Three breaches. Three kids she escalated because the math said escalate. You want to know what happened to those kids?"

Javi didn't answer.

"Me neither. That's not my job." The way you'd say *I don't handle catering*. Then, quieter: "But I do know their names are in a database somewhere. Funny how that works."

Typing. "Capital allocation. Returns. You're at 42nd percentile. Trending down. I'm redeeming. Eighty-seven K. Thirty days."

"I'll need to sell retained equity—"

"Your concern."

"—or take a loan against expected value—"

"Also your concern."

"—or file a breach on an underperforming contract."

A pause. Warmth, or a performance of it. "Now you're thinking like a businessman. Thirty days. Good talking to you, Javi."

14:47.

He sat in twelve square meters looking at ₱1.41M across twenty-three contracts, most of it locked until someone graduated or hit a milestone. Outside, a courier arguing in the lot, rapid Tagalog. AC rattling. He muted the TCL before the talking heads could explain trickle-down.

He scrolled past the red. Stopped on the first green.

Maria R., sixteen, Month 11. Blue Ateneo Senior High. Scholarship three months ago. Her mom last week: midterm photo, all blues, one word. *Salamat.*

Intake: single mom, father in Saudi, remittances irregular. Skipping school. ₱90K covered arrears and a year of tutoring. Hours on video, applications, a study group, the interview.

If she got UP, lifetime jumped. His twelve percent would be worth almost ₱400K.

He looked at *Salamat* again. Almost as good.

Then he did the thing instead of thinking about the thing.

Attribution Lending Inc. Thirty-day bridge, 18% APR, guild-certified. Application already half-filled from last time he'd opened it and walked away.

He opened a candidate file. Thirteen years old. School uniform, red-and-white plaid. Smiling. Did not know she was a vehicle.

He closed her.

Opened the lending app. Thumb on submit. The progress wheel. Confirmation to his glasses before he'd decided he'd decided.

Loan against expected value. Not a breach. Not this afternoon.

His hands were still a little unsteady. He put the Vizor back on the dock.

---

The night before the Korr broadcast, Rosa signed CarePath at 22:34. ₱145,000 hit the account. Lianne's GlucoSync, two years. Marco's door stayed closed.

She didn't know that in thirteen hours, everything would change.

None of them did.
