# The Shift
*Thursday, April 17, 2050 — Seoul National University Hospital, Bundang*

---

The man came in at 01:14, clutching his left arm like it owed him money.

Seo-jin saw him from across the ER—through the controlled chaos of a Thursday night in Bundang, past the motorcycle accident in Bay 3 and the grandmother in Bay 7 whose blood pressure was doing something creative and the medical student in the corner who was trying to start an IV with the particular concentration of someone defusing a bomb. She saw the man and her body knew before her mind did. The way he held himself. The color under his eyes. The tightness in his jaw that wasn't pain—pain made people grimace, made them move, made them *seek*. This was something else. This was a man whose body was shutting down and who didn't have anyone to tell.

"Bay 2. Now." She was already moving. Her nunchi—the eye-measure, the body-knowledge, the thing the monitors couldn't see—had read him in three seconds. Her conscious mind caught up in five.

His name was Park Jae-won. Seventy-one. Retired. Former engineer at a company that had been absorbed into Samsung Engineering two decades ago. Lived alone in a villa in Yatap-dong—one of the older neighborhoods, the ones built during the first Bundang development wave in the '90s, before the planned city had planned itself quite as thoroughly. No emergency contact listed. The system flagged this: **Next of kin: None designated. Social connection index: 12th percentile.**

Twelfth percentile. Meaning he was more isolated than 88% of the population. The II tracked this the way it tracked everything—dispassionately, accurately, without prejudice. It was a data point. It was also a man arriving in her ER at one in the morning with nobody to call.

"어디가 아프세요?" *Where does it hurt?*

"왼쪽 팔. 어깨부터." *Left arm. From the shoulder.* His voice was steady. Too steady—the steadiness of someone who'd been alone long enough to learn that no one was coming to help, so you might as well be calm.

She ran the diagnostics. Troponin levels elevated. ST segment showing the first whispers of something wrong—not screaming yet, but whispering, the way a foundation whispers before it cracks. Her hands moved through the examination with the efficiency of eight years in emergency medicine, but her nunchi was doing the real work. His breathing was shallow but controlled. His skin was cool—not cold, not the cold of circulatory collapse, but the cool of someone who'd been sitting alone in an apartment deciding whether this was worth a hospital visit. His eyes tracked her movements with an intelligence that the social connection index couldn't see.

"언제부터 아팠어요?" *How long has it been hurting?*

A pause. "며칠." *A few days.*

"며칠이면 몇 일?" *When you say a few days, how many?*

Another pause. Longer. "나흘." *Four.*

Four days. He'd been having cardiac symptoms for four days and hadn't come in. Seo-jin's face didn't change—you learned in emergency medicine not to show the reaction, because the patient would read it and panic, and panic was bad for hearts that were already struggling. But inside, she felt the familiar anger. Not at him. At the gap. The gap between what the system could see and what it couldn't.

The II had his health metrics. Had his activity data, his pharmacy records, his hospital history. The wellness algorithms should have flagged declining cardiac indicators days ago. Maybe they had. Maybe the notification had gone to a dashboard nobody checked, or to a phone he'd silenced because notifications from systems that tracked you without knowing you felt more like surveillance than care. The system was designed for people with people—family members who'd notice, friends who'd ask *밥 먹었어?*, someone in the social lattice who'd translate the algorithm's flag into a human question: *Are you okay?*

Park Jae-won had no one to translate.

"IV access. Heparin drip. Get cardiology on the line." She kept her voice level. The medical student appeared—the one from the corner, looking relieved to have a task that wasn't the impossible IV. "Get a 12-lead EKG and don't let anyone move him."

She worked. The ER hummed around her—the particular sound of Korean hospital efficiency, quieter than the American ERs she'd done her exchange rotation in, louder than the Japanese ones she'd visited for a conference. Korean hospitals occupied a specific acoustic space: the beep of monitors in a minor key, the squeak of shoes on sealed concrete, the low rapid Korean of nurses conferring in a register designed to be authoritative without alarming patients. It was the sound of a system functioning. The sound of attribution metrics being generated with every intervention, every medication administered, every outcome recorded.

The II logged her actions in real-time. Intervention timestamp. Drug selection. Dosage. Response latency. All of it feeding into her attribution score—the number that determined her salary, her research funding, her professional standing. Last quarter: 91st percentile. Emergency medicine, nationwide. Her save rate was 97.3%. The number was accurate. It reflected genuine skill, genuine training, genuine value. The system saw her clearly.

It just couldn't see what made the number possible. Couldn't see the three seconds where her nunchi had read Park Jae-won from across the ER before a single monitor beeped. Couldn't see the years of pattern recognition that lived in her muscles rather than her charts. Couldn't see the thing she'd learned from halmeoni without either of them knowing it was being transmitted—that survival was a frequency you learned to hear, and that hearing it was not a metric but a gift.

The troponin peaked at 02:40. Cardiology confirmed: NSTEMI. Non-ST elevation myocardial infarction. Serious but catchable. Treatable. The kind of event that killed people who came in four days too late and saved people who came in four hours too late. Park Jae-won had arrived in the narrow window between dying and living, and the fact that the window existed at all was a function of cardiac care that worked, and hospital funding that worked, and an attribution system that paid Seo-jin enough to be here at one in the morning.

By 03:30, he was stable. Catheterization scheduled for the morning. The cardiologist would place a stent, open the artery, send him home in three days with a medication regimen and a follow-up schedule.

And then what?

Seo-jin stood at the nurses' station, filling in the discharge planning form that would determine what happened when Park Jae-won left the hospital. The form had a field for **Post-Discharge Support Network**. She looked at his chart. No next of kin. Social connection index: 12th percentile. The community care system would flag him automatically—the II's welfare algorithms would dispatch a check-in within 48 hours of discharge, assign a care coordinator, ensure medication compliance. The system had infrastructure for this. It had attribution for this. It worked.

But the form didn't have a field for: *Who will ask this man if he's eaten?*

She filled in what she could. Flagged him for the enhanced welfare protocol—the one that added human contact beyond the algorithmic minimums. Wrote a note: **Patient presents with significant social isolation. Recommend community integration referral. Cardiac rehabilitation should include social wellness component.**

The note would be read by someone. Acted on by someone. The system would catch him. Probably. Most likely. The metrics said the enhanced protocol improved outcomes by 22%.

Twenty-two percent was good. Twenty-two percent was not a grandmother producing japchae from a dimensional pocket.

She walked down the hallway toward the break room. The shift was four hours from ending. She had two more patients to check, a research meeting at 09:00 that she'd attend on caffeine and spite, and a half-written grant application on her laptop at home that was due Monday.

The research. Her other life—the one that existed between shifts, in the hours that should have been sleep. The cardiac treatment she'd been developing for three years: a nanoscale delivery mechanism for targeted cardiac repair. Not a drug—a delivery system. A way to get existing cardiac medications to the exact cells that needed them, reducing dosage by 90% and side effects by 80%. The science was elegant. She was genuinely proud of it.

The patent would belong to the hospital consortium. Standard practice—institutional research, institutional IP. The consortium would license it to pharmaceutical manufacturers. The licensing fees would fund more research. The cycle was efficient, transparent, well-attributed.

And the treatment would cost ₩7,200,000 per course. About $5,400. Affordable in Seoul, in Tokyo, in Copenhagen. Unaffordable in Manila, in Dhaka, in Lagos.

She knew this. Had known it since the first licensing projections came back. Had done the math during a conference in Manila last year—the APAC Healthcare Innovation Summit, held at the Philippine International Convention Center, where the air conditioning worked too hard and the coffee was terrible and the attendees were divided, visibly, by a line that tracked almost perfectly with national GDP per capita: the people who could afford the treatments they were discussing, and the people who could not.

A nurse from Manila General had asked the question during the panel Q&A. Seo-jin had been presenting her delivery mechanism research—preliminary results, promising data, the careful optimism of a scientist who knew the numbers were good but wouldn't say "breakthrough" until the replication studies confirmed it.

The nurse—Rosa, the name badge had said, though Seo-jin hadn't retained it at the time, had only remembered later, in the way you remember the face of someone who asked you a question you couldn't answer—raised her hand.

"The licensing model. What does this mean for hospitals like ours? For countries that will never be able to afford this?"

Seo-jin had said something about R&D cost recovery and technology diffusion curves and the historical pattern of medical innovations becoming affordable over time. The words had come easily. They were true. They were also—and she knew this as she said them, knew it in her body the way she knew when a patient was dying—insufficient. The answer was correct and the question was about something the answer couldn't reach.

The nurse had nodded politely. Had not asked a follow-up. The politeness was worse than anger would have been.

Seo-jin had thought about it on the flight home—thirteen hours to Incheon, the sleepless wakefulness of a red-eye where your body was in one timezone and your conscience was in another. Had thought about halmeoni's factory, where the product flowed one direction and the money flowed the other and the women in between weren't supposed to have opinions. Had thought about how the licensing model was not the factory, was not gapjil, was not Bae with his bamboo stick—but had the same shape when you looked at it from Manila instead of Seoul. Value created in one place, captured in another, the people in between paying for the privilege of benefiting from someone else's innovation.

She'd told herself: the system works. The attribution is transparent. The licensing funds research that produces more innovations that eventually become affordable. The development ladder. The Coalbrookdale argument—she'd never heard it called that, but she knew the shape of it, had absorbed it the way every Korean absorbed it, because the development ladder was Korea's national mythology, the story the country told itself about how bark porridge became yakgwa.

The argument was true. It was also, at 03:45 on a Thursday morning in Bundang, standing in a hallway that smelled like antiseptic and recycled air, looking at a discharge form for a man who had no one to ask him if he'd eaten—the argument was also a thing she carried the way halmeoni carried the cardigan. Not because she needed it. Because she couldn't put it down.

---

Home. Mapo-gu. 08:12.

The apartment was small and hers and exactly what she needed, which was the first and last thing she appreciated about it. Thirty-two pyeong—about a hundred square meters—in a mid-rise on a side street off Wausan-ro, close enough to Hongdae that she could hear the music on weekends and far enough that she didn't have to participate. She'd bought it four years ago with a conventional mortgage at 3.2%—no jeonse, no deposit nightmare, no landlord who might or might not return her life savings. The II's property registry made the transaction transparent: construction cost, land value, municipal tax, maintenance fund. No speculative premium. No information asymmetry. She'd known exactly what she was buying and exactly what it was worth. Her grandmother had wept when she signed the papers. Not from happiness—from relief. From the memory of what housing meant when housing was a weapon.

The apartment smelled like doenjang-jjigae—the batch she'd made Sunday night after visiting halmeoni, the fermented soybean paste bubbling with tofu and zucchini and potatoes and the depth of flavor that came from the doenjang she bought from the ajumma at Mangwon Market who made it herself in clay pots on a rooftop in Hapjeong. The jjigae was on its fourth day, which meant it was approaching peak flavor—doenjang-jjigae was one of those things that improved with age, that rewarded patience, that tasted best when it had sat with itself long enough to become more than the sum of its ingredients.

She heated a bowl. Sat at the kitchen table. The table was covered with medical journals—the *New England Journal of Medicine*, the *Korean Journal of Emergency Medicine*, a stack of printed-out preprints on nanoscale drug delivery that she'd been annotating with a red pen because some things were better done by hand. Her laptop was open to the grant application: **Targeted Cardiac Repair via Nanoscale Delivery Mechanism: Phase III Clinical Trial Proposal.** The cursor blinked at Section 4.2: Projected Licensing Revenue.

She ate. The doenjang-jjigae was good. The jjigae was always good. It was the one constant in a life that ran on thirty-two-hour shifts and cold coffee and the adrenaline of not knowing what would walk through the ER doors next.

Her phone sat face-up on the table. A news banner scrolled across the top: **KORR FOUNDATION ANNOUNCES GLOBAL BROADCAST — APRIL 18 — "HUMANITY'S NEXT CHAPTER."** She'd seen the promos for weeks. Everyone had. The richest man on Earth, the architect of the II, the reason her hospital was funded and her apartment was affordable and her grandmother was warm—Damon Korr was going to tell the world something. The speculation ranged from new governance protocols to expanded life extension to the persistent rumor, never confirmed, that BioGenesis had cracked consciousness transfer.

If the rumor was true—if consciousness could survive death—then Park Jae-won's NSTEMI was a footnote. Cardiac failure would be a temporary inconvenience. Death itself would be optional.

Seo-jin thought about this the way she thought about everything: clinically first, then personally, then not at all. Clinically: consciousness transfer would restructure emergency medicine entirely. Her specialty would become palliative rather than salvific—managing the transition rather than preventing the outcome. Personally: halmeoni would not choose it. Seo-jin was certain of this with the certainty of someone who knew her grandmother's relationship with death, which was not fear but familiarity, the way you know an old neighbor who you'd rather not invite in but who you'd acknowledge on the street. 그래도 살았지—*but still, I lived*—was a statement about living, not about not-dying. There was a difference.

She dismissed the notification. Whatever the richest man on Earth wanted to announce, it wouldn't change her shift schedule. Or Park Jae-won's isolation. Or the cost of her cardiac treatment in Manila.

She called halmeoni. 08:23. Early, but halmeoni had been awake since five—had been awake since five every day since 1951, when sleeping late meant missing the food distribution.

"서진아, 왜 이 시간에?" *Why are you calling at this hour?*

"방금 퇴근했어요. 할머니, 밥 먹었어요?" *Just got off shift. Halmeoni, have you eaten?*

"내가 안 먹겠어? 항상 먹지." *Would I not eat? I always eat.* The familiar answer. The answer that meant: I survived the bark porridge. I survived the factory. I survived the jeonse loss and the gapjil and the husband dying on a Tuesday. Of course I've eaten. Eating is the minimum. Eating is what you do when you've decided to keep going.

"오늘 환자 한 분 있었는데—" *There was a patient today—*

"혼자 사는 사람?" *Someone who lives alone?*

Seo-jin paused. "어떻게 알았어요?" *How did you know?*

"네 목소리에서 알지. 걱정하는 목소리야." *I can hear it in your voice. That's your worried voice.* A pause. Softer: "서진아, 다 못 구해. 알지?" *Seo-jin-ah, you can't save everyone. You know that?*

"알아요."

"근데 그래도 해보는 거지." *But still, you try.* 

그래도.

The word again. *But still.* The conjunction that held together the entire grammar of Korean survival. But still I lived. But still I try. But still I drove to Daejeon for yakgwa on a Sunday. But still I call my grandmother at eight in the morning because a man in my ER had no one to call. But still, but still, but still—the word that refused to let the sentence end at the hard part.

"자." Her grandmother's voice was firm. *Sleep.* "내일 또 구해야 하잖아." *You have to save people again tomorrow.*

Seo-jin hung up. Finished the jjigae. Washed the bowl—by hand, because some things were better done by hand, because the act of washing a bowl was a small bridge between the hospital's measured efficiency and the unmeasured life she lived inside it.

She looked at the grant application. Section 4.2. Projected licensing revenue. The numbers that would determine how many people could afford to not die of the thing she'd learned to prevent.

She closed the laptop.

Tomorrow was the Korr broadcast. The whole world watching. Whatever it was—consciousness transfer, new protocols, expanded life extension—it would be about systems. About measurement. About the machinery of civilization optimizing itself toward some new frontier.

She thought about Park Jae-won alone in Bay 2. About halmeoni saying *the system made the space; Eunji filled it*. About the nurse in Manila whose name she hadn't remembered at the time and which now, at 08:34 on a Thursday morning in Mapo-gu, she suddenly wished she had.

She went to bed. Slept badly. Dreamed about bark porridge and the cost of cardiac treatments and a man in an ER holding his left arm like it owed him money, and woke knowing she'd forgotten something important that she'd only remember later, when it was too late to matter.

Tomorrow, the world would change.

She set her alarm for 10:30 and closed her eyes.
