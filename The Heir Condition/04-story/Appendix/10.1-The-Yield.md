# The Yield

*In Seoul, Dr. Yoon Seo-jin continued to practice emergency medicine at Bundang Hospital for another twenty-three years. Her targeted cardiac delivery mechanism—released to commons on April 18, 2050—became the global standard of care within five years. By 2060, the treatment was manufactured in fourteen countries, at an average cost of $9.40 per dose. Post-cascade estimates attributed 2.3 million lives saved in the first decade alone, though Seo-jin herself dismissed the number when it was cited in her presence: "Attribution is for systems. The lives are for the people who lived them."*

*The Quezon City manufacturing facility, established through her collaboration with Dr. Reyes at UP Manila, became the largest cardiac treatment producer in Southeast Asia. The Filipino engineers who adapted her mechanism for tropical supply chains published forty-seven papers in the process, each one an innovation in its own right, each one in commons. The development ladder, it turned out, could also be a development web—connections running laterally, not just vertically, when the intermediaries weren't there to direct the traffic.*

*Yoon Soon-ok died in her sleep at the Bundang Silver Community on March 3, 2054, at the age of ninety-three. The cause was listed as cardiac failure—the ordinary kind, the kind that comes from a heart that has been beating for ninety-three years and has decided, quietly and without drama, that it has beaten enough. Eunji was on shift. She had brought sujeonggwa that morning—not because it was Tuesday, but because she'd noticed, with the particular attentiveness of someone who had spent six years learning the rhythms of this specific woman, that Soon-ok had seemed tired the evening before.*

*Soon-ok's final words, according to the facility's care log, were directed at her granddaughter over the phone at 06:14 that morning: "밥 먹었어?" Have you eaten?*

*Seo-jin had not eaten. She was in the middle of a shift. She ate afterward.*

*The care facility held a small memorial. Fourteen residents attended. Eunji spoke. Dohyun sang a trot ballad that Soon-ok had particularly loved—an old song about a woman waiting for a train that never comes, which had made Soon-ok laugh every time because, she said, "waiting for trains that don't come is the most Korean thing I've ever heard." The attribution system logged the memorial as a community wellness event. It did not log the laughter, or the sujeonggwa, or the fact that a woman who had survived a war and a factory and a stolen savings pool and a husband who died on a Tuesday was remembered, in the end, by people who had jeong with her. The system made the space. The people filled it.*

*On Soon-ok's bedside table, found during the room's clearing, was a half-eaten yakgwa from Sungsimdang bakery in Daejeon—chrysanthemum-pressed, honey-soaked, the color of old amber. Her granddaughter had brought it the previous Sunday, as she had every Sunday for eleven years. The cookie was not donated to any museum. It was discarded with the ordinary waste of an ordinary room in an ordinary care facility, because some things have no significance beyond the people who shared them, and that is exactly the right amount of significance.*
