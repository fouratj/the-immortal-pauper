# Chapter 11: Sunday
*Sunday, October 16, 2050 — Manila / Bundang*

---

The barangay hall in Tondo smelled like instant coffee and floor wax.

Javi got there at eight to open the windows before the heat arrived. The hall lent him the back room on Sundays for nothing, which was fortunate, because nothing was approximately his budget. The room held eleven plastic chairs, a whiteboard he'd bought secondhand from a closed tutoring franchise—the ghost of someone's growth-mindset poster still adhesived to the back—and a box fan that worked if you hit it.

The Vizor Pro 3 was in a drawer at his apartment. He'd tried to sell it in June; the resale market for surveillance glasses had collapsed along with the industry that needed them, and in the end he'd kept it in the way you keep the uniform of an army that lost. No body camera. No dashboards. No baselines. When he needed to know how one of his kids was doing, he had to do the thing that four years of guild certification had trained out of him: ask, and then sit there, unrecording, while the answer took as long as it took.

They came in ones and twos through the morning. Kids out of the MentorMaxx facilities, mostly—the ones who'd walked out in April when the machine went quiet, into families that didn't know what to do with what came back. Nobody's algorithm had a category for them. That was the whole clientele: people below resolution threshold.

The new attribution parameters, it turned out, could see some of it. Crisis intervention, mentorship, the unglamorous work of sitting with a sixteen-year-old until the shaking stopped—inference weights at 0.85 meant the system now guessed at the value of things it couldn't directly count, and the guesses landed in his account in amounts that would have made his old investors laugh. It came to less than a third of what he'd made buying children's futures. It was the first money he'd ever earned that he didn't check twice a day.

Marco Santos arrived at ten, the way he had every Sunday since August.

He didn't run sessions. Nobody would have used that word. He sat in the circle of plastic chairs with kids who had been where he'd been, and when one of them said the thing about how the facility staff were polite, how that was the worst part, the politeness, Marco said *yeah* with the full weight of someone who wasn't reading it off a slide, and the room changed. Javi had watched it happen a dozen times and had stopped trying to figure out which attribution category it belonged in. Some things you just fund with your own life.

Carlos showed up at noon with lumpia his mother had made, which was the tax Luz levied on the whole arrangement: her son could spend Sundays in Tondo if Tondo got fed. He was fifteen now and had opinions about everything and owed Javi nothing—the contract voided six months ago by a stranger's billion dollars—and he kept coming anyway, which Javi had decided not to examine too closely in case it stopped.

"Kumusta?" Javi asked him, because it was still the only question worth anything.

"Okay lang." Carlos dropped into a chair, stole a lumpia from his own tribute. "You look tired, pare."

"I'm broke and I run a support group in a barangay hall. I'm allowed."

"You were more broke before," Carlos said, with the terrible accuracy of the young. "You just had better glasses."

Rico came by at the end of the day, in his Megawide shirt, concrete dust still on his forearms—the construction sector had been hiring like the end of a war since the housing patents opened, and he'd discovered, to no one's surprise but his own, that he was good at it. He stood in the doorway and watched the last kids stack the chairs. He never sat in the circle. He came, Javi understood, to verify. To stand in the doorway of a room that existed where the machine used to be, touch the scars on his shoulder, and leave without saying much.

"Same time next week?" Javi asked.

"I'm pouring foundations in Caloocan next week." Rico paused at the door. "Yeah. Same time."

---

Rosa worked the day shift now.

That was the sentence, and she was still not used to the size of it. Days. The GlucoSync cost ₱340; the terror arithmetic that had structured eight years—rent or blood sugar, sleep or overtime, the night differential that paid for the mornings she missed—had simply been deleted, and what remained was a life she had to relearn how to live at the pace of an ordinary tired person. Her attribution score had climbed forty places when the inference weights started crediting the thing she'd always done—reading Mrs. Reyes's lips from the doorway—but she'd left the public visibility off. Some refusals you kept, on principle, even after the reason for them was gone.

On the third Sunday of October, St. Agnes Parish held its fiesta fundraiser, and the choir director—who had heard her humming over the dishes at the last one—committed an ambush with a microphone.

She sang "Ang Huling El Bimbo." The Eraserheads version, not the cover.

The parish hall was folding tables and streamers and a sound system with one blown speaker, and none of it mattered, because she hit the bridge and her throat opened the way it had opened once in 2038 and never since, and the room went quiet. Not polite-quiet. Listening-quiet. Lianne stopped mid-run with two other eight-year-olds and stared at her mother like she'd revealed a superpower, which was accurate. Marco stood at the back with the lumpia tray and did not film it. He'd thought about it—his hand had actually moved toward his phone—and then he'd put the phone away and just watched, because he was seventeen and had spent six months as a data stream and knew better than anyone in that room the difference between a recording and a memory.

Twelve years, she thought, walking home through the gray-gold evening with her daughter swinging between her and Marco, one hand each. The category with the closed door—the singing, the watercolors, the version of her life with room in it. The door had been there the whole time. It had just needed the weight taken off it.

Second place, again, in the informal reckoning of the fiesta. Aling Nena's granddaughter had done a Sarah Geronimo medley with choreography; there was no competing with choreography.

Rosa didn't mind. She had stopped keeping score in systems she didn't choose.

The Bundang Silver Community smelled like sujeonggwa and floor wax.

Seo-jin signed in at 14:02—the biometric pad logging her arrival, cross-referencing a schedule that no longer alarmed it; she'd worked a human number of hours this week, her department funded at rates that finally matched what emergency medicine had always been worth. The paper bag from Sungsimdang was warm against her ribs. She'd driven to Daejeon that morning with the windows down.

Room C-14. She knocked and didn't wait. "할머니, 저예요."

"Why do you knock? I heard the frog machine." Soon-ok was in the good chair by the window, English crossword on her lap, wearing the cardigan. October light lay across the photographs: the wedding, the unsmiling soldier, the refugee camp stretching to the Busan harbor with a seven-year-old somewhere in it, carrying the future on her back.

They ate the yakgwa in the established order: inspected, assessed, approved. Honey soak, sesame distribution, chrysanthemum geometry. Since 1956.

"News," Seo-jin said, in the shorthand of their Sundays. "The Quezon City facility shipped its first production run this week. Dr. Reyes sent a photograph." She held up her phone: a loading dock in Manila, pallets of white cartons, a dozen people crowded into frame making victory signs, and in the middle a woman in a UP Cardiology polo laughing at something outside the shot. "Eleven dollars a dose. We got it under fourteen. The humidity reformulation actually improved the stability profile—Reyes's team found it, not mine. It's in their name."

Her grandmother studied the photograph for a long time. A cardiac treatment, manufactured in the tropics, by a team assembled from strangers, for the price of a good meal—flowing now toward every country that used to be described, in the papers Seo-jin no longer had to write, as *cost-prohibitive markets*.

"The consortium people," Soon-ok said. "The ones who scattered."

"Dr. Min runs data for three commons projects out of the same office. The Boston postdoc landed at Reyes's lab, of all places—his visa cleared in August. Professor Abe is still suing everyone." Seo-jin shrugged. "The team didn't survive. The people did. They're just... recombined."

"Mm." Her grandmother handed back the phone and picked up another yakgwa. "Like after the war. Nothing keeps its shape. Everything keeps its people, if you're lucky." She chewed, considering. "You know a man visited me. In the spring."

"You mentioned." Six times, with new details on each pass, the way the war stories worked—memory as a living thing, changing shape depending on which part you pressed. "The one from the television."

"He ate all the japchae." This remained, in six tellings, the load-bearing fact—more notable than the resurrection, the trillions, the empire lost between one Tuesday and the next. A guest with an appetite. "He sent a letter last month. Paper. Someone taught him manners in his second life." She nodded at the wardrobe, where a cream envelope stood tucked into the mirror frame, next to a hand-tinted wedding photograph. "He writes that he is learning the slow way. Whatever that means to a man like that."

"What did you write back?"

"That the slow way is the only way, and he should eat more." She reached for her tea. "He's very intelligent. It gets in his way. Like some other people I feed."

The afternoon settled over the room. The relay in the ceiling blinked its green pulse every four seconds, logging what it could—the visit, the vitals, the medication schedule—while the rest of the hour ran on the older protocol: two women, one table, the light moving slowly across seventy years of photographs. Somewhere in Manila a hall full of plastic chairs was being stacked by kids the machine used to own. Somewhere in Toledo a daughter was framing a letter that never explained itself. Somewhere over the Pacific, or in a lab, or across a chessboard from an empty chair, the two richest paupers alive were learning, one move a month, what everyone in this room had always known.

"서진아."

"네?"

"밥 먹었어?"

The question arrived the way it always arrived. The oldest technology. Ten thousand years of infrastructure in three syllables, still running, still uncounted, still holding up the world.

"아직요."

"미쳤어." Already rising, eighty-nine years old, moving with the determination of a woman for whom this had never once been a small matter. Already reaching into the fold in space-time where the japchae lived. "Sit. Sit."

Seo-jin sat.

밥 먹었어?

네, 먹었어요.
