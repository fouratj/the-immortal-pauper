# Archive Document: The Bangalore Review
*Recovered from Korr Foundation Internal Records*  
*March 3, 2049 — 14 months before the Event*  
*Participants: Damon Korr, Dr. Yuki Tanaka (Chief Attribution Architect), Kaelen Korr (Observer, Estate Planning)*

---

The conference room on the 47th floor overlooking San Francisco Bay had been designed to make visitors feel small. Floor-to-ceiling windows framing the Pacific. A table of polished obsidian that cost more than most houses. Twelve empty chairs arranged around a man who needed no entourage to fill a room.

Damon Korr sat at the head of the table, reviewing the proposal on his tablet. Dr. Tanaka stood at the presentation display, hands clasped, waiting. In the corner, taking notes he would never file, Kaelen watched.

"Walk me through it again," Damon said. "The edge case."

Dr. Tanaka pulled up the visualization—a cascade tree showing attribution flows in late-adopter economies. "The backpropagation algorithm over-credits intermediary nodes in regions with fragmented infrastructure. When institutional layers are dense—multiple contractors, licensing aggregators, verification services—the algorithm attributes value to the intermediation itself rather than the underlying labor."

"Example."

"Care work in Manila. A home health aide named Rosa Dela Cruz." Tanaka highlighted a single node in the tree. "The II calculates her uplift contribution—the measurable increase in her patients' economic output after recovery, attributed back to her care. Forty thousand pesos monthly. But her employment contract assigns 100% of that attribution to Manila General Hospital. Standard clause. She receives a fixed wage: 9,000 pesos."

"And the hospital?"

"Keeps her attribution, pays it upstream to intermediaries. Equipment licensing. Training certification. Administrative overhead." Tanaka paused. "The same pattern appears across 847 documented cases in our late-adopter sample. Care workers, agricultural laborers, artisanal manufacturers. Employers capture worker attribution through standard contracts. The algorithm enables it by making individual attribution claims easy to waive—and caps them at 5% of total output even when not waived."

Damon set down the tablet. His face was unreadable—the expression Kaelen had learned to recognize as intense calculation.

"The proposed fix?"

"Parameter adjustments to the attribution metadata." Tanaka pulled up the next slide. "Three changes: First, raise individual attribution caps from 5% to 15% of calculated uplift. Second, increase inference weight for non-institutional contributions from 0.25x to 0.6x. Third, add minimum worker attribution floors—employers cannot capture more than 70% through contracts." <!-- let's add a small explanation of diffusion of tokens throughout the economy and how it helps, increase velocity of money blah blah -->

She advanced the slide. "Implementation cost: 0.02% of annual operating budget. Attribution correction across 47 countries affecting approximately 340 million primary laborers. We call it the Bangalore Patch. Test deployment showed workers receiving 35-40% of their calculated contribution instead of 15-20%."

Silence.

Kaelen watched his uncle's hands. Perfectly still on the obsidian table. The same hands that had taught him chess, that had held him at his mother's funeral.

"What's the failure mode?" Damon asked.

"Sir?"

"If we deploy this patch, what breaks?"

Tanaka hesitated. "Some intermediary institutions would see reduced attribution flows. Licensing aggregators, administrative services, verification bodies. Entities that have structured their operations around the current—"

"Around the current assumption that intermediation creates value," Damon finished. "Which your patch would call into question."

"The patch corrects a measurement error. It doesn't call anything into question except the error itself."

"Doesn't it?" Damon stood. Walked to the window. The Pacific stretched to the horizon, afternoon light fracturing across the water. "If we admit the algorithm over-credited intermediation in Manila, we're admitting it may have over-credited intermediation everywhere. Every licensing structure. Every administrative layer. Every attribution flow that passes through institutional complexity on its way to the foundational layer."

He turned.

The room was very quiet.

"The patch is technically correct," Damon said. "I don't dispute Dr. Tanaka's analysis. Rosa Dela Cruz probably does deserve more attribution than she receives. The evidence supports that conclusion."

"Then—"

"But deploying it creates precedent. Increases risks elsewhere. If we raise individual caps and lower employer capture in Manila, we're admitting the parameters were wrong. Every late-adopter country will demand their own adjustments. Every labor constituency will argue for higher inference weights, lower institutional priority." He walked to the window. "And if institutional attribution is systematically overcredited in Manila—maybe it's overcredited everywhere. Maybe the entire parameter set is wrong."

He turned back. "Including the foundational layer itself."

Tanaka went still.

"The patch is technically correct," Damon repeated. "But it questions the logic that justified these parameters in the first place. The logic that says institutional contribution should be weighted higher than individual contribution."

"With respect, sir, the current parameters *are* political. You set them. Unilaterally. In 2033."

Damon's expression didn't change, but something flickered in his eyes.

"And they've produced twenty years of unprecedented global prosperity," he said, voice still gentle. "Imperfect, yes. But stable. Predictable. Optimized within known parameters."

"Rosa Dela Cruz generates ₱40,000 uplift monthly," Tanaka said, voice hardening. "She receives ₱9,000. That's not an edge case. That's 340 million workers being systematically undercompensated because the parameters favor institutional capture."

"The parameters reflect institutional contribution—"

"The parameters reflect *your* contribution estimate from 2033. You decided institutions deserve unlimited claims. You decided individuals cap at 5%. You decided inference gets 0.25x weight." Tanaka took a step forward. "And now I'm asking you—as your Chief Architect, with the fix already built—to update those parameters. Point-zero-two percent of operating budget. Four hundred twelve lines of metadata. That's all it takes."

The silence stretched.

"You can do this," she continued. "Right now. Foundation authority. No governance vote required. Just update the reference parameters and publish. By tomorrow, 340 million workers would see fairer attribution. You don't need consensus. You need to type your signature and press commit."

Damon picked up the tablet. Handed it back to her.

"If I update parameters unilaterally—even to correct an error—I'm admitting that one person *can* unilaterally control attribution. That's precisely what democratic oversight was designed to prevent. The parameters should be set by governance consensus, not by me."

"But you SET them originally—"

"Which is why I won't set them again." His voice was final. "If you believe these parameters are wrong, Dr. Tanaka, propose changes through the governance process. Submit them to the II Global Council. Build consensus. Get democratic approval. That's how the system is supposed to work."

Tanaka stared at him. "That process takes eighteen months minimum." <!-- let's have a little story about power here. I like how Abraham Lincoln would always respond to a question with a story and an allegory -->

"Then convince the council to expedite." Damon's tone was maddeningly reasonable. "I'm not the bottleneck here. Democratic governance is. And I won't undermine democratic process—even for a good cause—because undermining it *once* destroys its legitimacy forever."

He picked up the tablet. Closed the proposal with a gesture that meant *declined, do not resurface*.

"The Bangalore Patch introduces uncertainty. Markets depend on predictable value flows. If we start adjusting attribution based on edge-case analysis, we undermine the foundational stability that makes the entire system function."

"And Rosa Dela Cruz?"

"Will benefit from the system's continued stability. As will her children, and their children. The aggregate outcome remains positive even if individual cases create hardship. That's what optimization means, Dr. Tanaka. Not perfection. Optimization."

Kaelen watched his uncle return to his seat. Watched him close the proposal with a gesture that meant *declined, do not resurface*.

Watched him not look at the corner where his nephew sat.

"Is there anything else?"

Dr. Tanaka stood very still for a moment. Then she gathered her materials.

"No, sir. Thank you for your time."

She left. The door closed with a soft click.

Damon turned to Kaelen. Smiled the warm smile that had comforted him through a hundred childhood nightmares.

"Estate planning notes?"

"Nothing relevant." Kaelen's voice came out steady. How was his voice steady? "Standard observation. I'll file it with the others."

"Good." Damon returned to his tablet. "Dinner Thursday? I've found a new chess variant I want to try on you."

"Thursday," Kaelen said. "I'll be there."

He walked out of the conference room on legs that felt like someone else's. Found a bathroom on the 44th floor. Locked the door.

And stood there for eleven minutes, staring at his reflection, understanding for the first time that his uncle was never going to change.

Not because he was blind.

Because he could see perfectly clearly, and had chosen to look away.

**March 21, 2049 - 11:34 PM**  
**Kaelen's Apartment, Palo Alto**

Three weeks after the Bangalore meeting, Kaelen woke at 2 AM with a question strangling him.

*What happens when the people who own everything stop dying?*

He'd been trying not to think about it. Trying to convince himself it wasn't a problem. That his uncle's refusal to deploy the Bangalore Patch was about stability, not extraction.

He couldn't do it anymore.

Kaelen got out of bed. The apartment was dark except for the city glow through the windows—Palo Alto at night, still humming with energy even at 2 AM. He walked to the kitchen. Made coffee. The Estonian blend from the Tallinn team. Strong enough to taste like a decision.

He sat at his workstation and stared at the blank screen.

If he did this—if he actually modeled what immortality would do to wealth concentration—his uncle would see it. The compute allocation would show up on next month's infrastructure report. Damon would ask questions.

But he needed to know.

He started typing.

---

The query took him forty minutes to formulate. Not because the question was complex—because he kept second-guessing one parameter.

**Include: Sociological dynamics, behavioral adaptation**

He typed it. Deleted it. Typed it again.

That was paranoid. The model didn't need sociology. This was economics. Wealth concentration. Simple math.

But if immortals and mortals diverged behaviorally—if they stopped thinking of each other as the same species—that mattered. That changed everything.

He deleted it again. Catastrophizing.

His finger hovered over the parameter line.

Fuck it. If he was going to burn 15 gigawatt-hours on paranoia, might as well be thorough.

He added it back.

**QUERY: Long-term systemic effects of selective immortality on wealth distribution**

**PARAMETERS:**
- Immortality cost: $4.7M (BioGenesis pricing)
- Eligible population: ~2.1M individuals (current wealth >$4.7M)
- Remaining population: 9.8B mortals (generational replacement, 80-year lifespan)
- Capital returns: 7% annually (historical average)
- Labor returns: 3% annually (wage growth)
- Include: Policy responses, taxation, democratic governance
- Include: Sociological dynamics, behavioral adaptation
- Include: Attribution layer effects (foundational 0.3% compounding)
- Time horizon: 500 years

**COMPUTE ALLOCATION: 15 gigawatt-hours**

He stared at the allocation. Fifteen gigawatt-hours. In a world that had finally achieved net-zero carbon in 2047, that was obscene. Most researchers got 0.5 gigawatt-hours per *month*. He was requesting thirty months' worth in one night.

The 2047 Climate Accords had been celebrated as humanity's greatest achievement. Net-zero. Carbon balanced. The planet saved.

What they didn't mention in the celebrations: net-zero meant *rationed*. Every watt consumed had to be offset. Solar and wind were intermittent. Fission worked but produced waste nobody wanted. The regulations were Byzantine—environmental reviews, waste disposal protocols, public approval processes that took years.

The result: energy became expensive. Scarce. Allocated.

AI development had cratered. The big training runs that had defined the 2030s—the ones that took 50 gigawatt-hours, 100 gigawatt-hours—became impossible. Too expensive. Too politically toxic. "Do we really need smarter chatbots more than we need to power hospitals?"

That's why the labor market looked like 2025 instead of 2050. That's why humans still did most knowledge work. That's why Rosa Dela Cruz worked twelve-hour shifts instead of being automated away.

Not because the technology didn't exist. Because running it cost more than paying humans.

The irony was perfect: environmental regulations had accidentally preserved human employment by making AI too expensive to deploy at scale.

But Kaelen had access. Family privilege. His uncle's private fission reactors—four of them, built before the Accords tightened permitting to impossibility. His uncle's carbon credits, purchased when they were cheap. His uncle's infrastructure, grandfathered into the old rules.

He could burn dozens of gigawatt-hours in one night and the only consequence would be a line item on next month's report.

The irony was thick: using aristocratic energy privilege to model what aristocratic privilege would become.

He clicked [Execute].

The system flagged it immediately:

*WARNING: This allocation exceeds standard research parameters*  
*Carbon cost: ₱584,000*  
*This usage will appear on monthly infrastructure reports*  
*Estimated runtime: 1 hour 47 minutes*

Kaelen's finger hovered over [Confirm].

₱584,000 in carbon credits. Enough to power 2,000 homes for a month. Enough to run a small hospital for a year.

All to satisfy a paranoid hunch he was probably wrong about.

His uncle would see it next month. Would ask: "What were you researching?"

What would he say? Estate planning? Economic modeling? Academic curiosity?

The truth was simpler: "I was checking if your immortality announcement next month will create a permanent aristocracy."

Damon would laugh. Would explain why Kaelen was being paranoid. Would generate some sophisticated reason why the concern was overblown.

Just like he'd done with the Bangalore Patch.

Kaelen realized: if the model showed nothing, he'd drop it. If the model confirmed his fears, he'd need to act. Either way, he needed to know.

And if Damon asked about the compute usage? He'd tell the truth. Show him the results. Give him one more chance to see it.

One more chance to prove Kaelen wrong.

He clicked [Confirm].

The progress bar appeared. His workstation's fans spun up, pulling power from the grid. Somewhere in Nevada, one of his uncle's private fission reactors adjusted its output ever so slightly—uranium rods sliding deeper into the reaction chamber, neutrons splitting atoms, heat converting to steam converting to electricity. The waste would accumulate in concrete casks, buried in the desert, radioactive for ten thousand years.

But that was someone else's problem. Someone else's future.

Somewhere in a database, his uncle's carbon credits began decrementing. Purchased in bulk in 2043, before the Accords made them expensive. Back when you could still burn energy without justifying every watt to a review board.

Kaelen made more coffee and waited.

**March 22, 2049 - 1:21 AM**

The results appeared on his screen.

Kaelen read them once. Twice. Three times.

**YEAR 100:** Top 2.1M immortals own 82% of global wealth  
**YEAR 200:** 94%  
**YEAR 500:** 99.2%

His hands were shaking. The coffee had gone cold.

But that wasn't what made him stop breathing.

It was the sociological analysis. The model's behavioral subroutines had flagged something at year 287:

**ALERT: Speciation event detected**  
**Description: Divergent value systems between immortal and mortal populations exceed historical precedent for intra-species cooperation**  
**Confidence: 0.89**

Speciation event. The model was saying that by year 287, immortals and mortals would have diverged so completely—different time horizons, different interests, different lived experiences—that they'd effectively become separate species.

He clicked into the details.

By year 300, the immortals had stopped thinking of mortals as fully human. Not out of malice. Out of incomprehension. How do you relate to beings who die after 80 years when you've been alive for 250? Who make decisions based on decades when you're playing a thousand-year game?

The model showed the immortals discussing "the mortal problem" the way humans discussed wildlife conservation. Habitat management. Sustainable population levels. Benevolent stewardship.

The immortals, in the model, had forgotten they were ever anything else.

Kaelen sat back in his chair. The apartment was very quiet. Outside, Palo Alto hummed with the sound of a city that never quite slept—distant traffic, the whir of delivery drones, the hum of infrastructure.

This couldn't be right. This had to be a modeling artifact. Some parameter he'd set wrong. Some assumption that was too pessimistic.

He needed to test it. Run variations. See if there was *any* scenario where this didn't happen.

He started building a scenario matrix. Every policy intervention he could think of. Every democratic response. Every combination of taxation, regulation, redistribution.

The system let him queue them up: 50 scenarios, 100, 150, 200...

He stopped at 247. That felt comprehensive. Every major policy lever. Every realistic intervention.

He clicked [Estimate Compute Requirements].

The system thought for a moment:

*WARNING: Scenario complexity requires iterative refinement*  
*Initial compute estimate: 35-90 gigawatt-hours*  
*Estimated runtime: 18-47 hours*  
*Family override required for allocations >50 GWh*

Kaelen stared at the warning.

35 to 90 gigawatt-hours. Maybe 50 total with the 15 he'd already spent. Maybe 105. The system couldn't tell him until it ran.

And his uncle would see anything over 50 on next month's report.

His finger hovered over [Execute].

Then he stopped.

If he ran this on his uncle's infrastructure, Damon would see it. Would ask to see the results. And Kaelen would show him: "Look, 90% of scenarios end in permanent aristocracy."

And Damon would do what he always did. Explain why the models were wrong. Why the assumptions were too pessimistic. Why the system would self-correct.

Just like the Bangalore Patch.

Kaelen's hand moved to [Cancel].

He needed compute that Damon couldn't see. Couldn't audit. Couldn't dismiss.

His uncle had built Research Annex Seven in 2041—separate facility, separate power, separate reporting. For "sensitive projects that require operational security." Damon had given Kaelen access two years ago: "Sometimes you need to model scenarios that would be misinterpreted if seen out of context."

The irony was perfect.

Kaelen booked it. 72 hours. The system logged it as: *K. Korr - Estate modeling - Confidential*

Would show up on quarterly audits eventually. But not for months.

**March 22, 2049 - 3:47 AM**  
**Korr Foundation Research Annex Seven, Location Undisclosed**

The facility was twenty meters underground. Climate controlled to 16°C—the temperature where server efficiency peaked. Cold enough that Kaelen's breath almost fogged.

It smelled like ozone and cold recycled air and his uncle's paranoia. The walls were bare concrete. The lighting was harsh fluorescent. No windows. No natural light. Just servers humming in the dark.

His uncle had built this place for asking questions that couldn't be public. For modeling scenarios that would be "misinterpreted."

Kaelen was using it to ask the question Damon had never thought to ask: *What happens when people like you live forever?*

He made coffee first. The facility had the good stuff—Ethiopian blend, his uncle's expensive stock. Probably the same coffee Damon drank when he sat in this bunker modeling his own sensitive scenarios.

The irony was thick enough to taste.

He sat at the terminal and loaded the scenario matrix. 247 scenarios. Every policy intervention. Every possible path.

The system would use what it needed. The facility had its own power. Its own reporting structure that wouldn't surface for months.

He clicked [Execute].

The servers spun up. The cooling system kicked in harder—compensating for the heat about to be generated. Somewhere in the facility's guts, power flowed from his uncle's private grid.

Kaelen drank coffee that tasted like expensive betrayal and waited.

**April 5, 2049 - 6:23 AM**

The results appeared after 41 hours of continuous computation.

**SCENARIOS TESTED: 247**  
**SCENARIOS PREVENTING PERMANENT ARISTOCRACY: 23 (9.3%)**  
**SCENARIOS RESULTING IN PERMANENT ARISTOCRACY: 224 (90.7%)**

Kaelen stared at the numbers. 23 out of 247. He needed to understand why those 23 worked.

"ARBITER. Show me the 23 as a dependency graph. What do they all require?"

The screen cleared. Rebuilt itself as a network—nodes and edges, each scenario connected to the conditions that enabled it. Too dense. Too complex.

"Simplify. Show me the root dependencies. What structural conditions must exist?"

The graph collapsed. Four nodes remained:

```
ROOT DEPENDENCIES (All 23 scenarios require some combination)

NODE-A: Unilateral authority over foundational attribution layer
NODE-B: Time-limited operational window  
NODE-C: Bypass of democratic consensus mechanisms
NODE-D: Legal framework discontinuity (system in undefined state)
```

Kaelen studied them. "What creates these conditions?"

*Insufficient data. These are structural requirements, not causal mechanisms.*

"Speculate."

A pause. Then:

*Speculation: NODE-A requires heir-level access or equivalent authority. NODE-B requires temporary power transfer. NODE-C requires operating during governance gap where consensus doesn't apply. NODE-D requires legal state that existing frameworks weren't designed to handle.*

Kaelen pulled up a separate window. Estonian inheritance protocols. Started scrolling.

Legal state that existing frameworks weren't designed to handle...

Section 47.3: Resurrection Clause.

He read it carefully. The Estonian designers had included it for wrongful death scenarios—if someone was declared dead by mistake and that error was later corrected. The clause allowed inheritance reversal.

But it assumed resurrection was error correction. Medical mistake. Identity fraud. Not... actual resurrection. Planned resurrection. Technological resurrection.

"ARBITER. Map this to the dependency nodes. Estonian inheritance protocols, Section 47.3."

The four nodes lit up with connections:

```
SECTION 47.3 ANALYSIS

Death certificate issuance → NODE-A (heir authority commences)
Death to resurrection window → NODE-B (temporary, not permanent)  
Inheritance is automatic → NODE-C (no democratic vote required)
Resurrection not in legal framework → NODE-D (undefined state)
```

All four nodes. One event.

Death. But not permanent death.

Kaelen sat back. The bunker's cooling system hummed.

"ARBITER. Show me this as worldlines. Physics model. Possible futures branching from present."

The screen rebuilt itself again. Not a dependency graph—a tree of branching paths, each one a trajectory through time. Like Feynman diagrams, but for civilizations instead of particles.

The visualization was solving the Bellman equation—dynamic programming across possible futures, calculating optimal paths through state space. Each worldline represented a trajectory where decisions at branch points determined which attractor basin you ended up in.

Most worldlines were red. The aristocracy futures. 224 of them, all converging toward the same attractor: permanent extraction, 99.2% wealth concentration by year 500. Like marbles rolling into a valley—once you're in the basin, escaping requires more energy than the system possesses.

23 worldlines were blue. The prevention futures. Most split from the present through impossible decision nodes—"Global Constitutional Amendment," "Revolutionary Expropriation," "Voluntary Abdication." These were escape trajectories. Like launching a rocket from the valley floor. Theoretically possible. Practically impossible without energy the system didn't have.

But one worldline—thin, fragile, barely visible—split from the present at a different point.

The decision node was labeled: **"Legal Death + Guaranteed Resurrection"**

Kaelen zoomed in.

This worldline didn't escape through energy. It exploited a discontinuity—a brief moment when the attractor basin's walls didn't exist. Like a quantum tunneling event. The marble didn't climb out of the valley. It slipped through a gap in the potential field that only existed for a moment.

From that node, the worldline ran blue for a brief period—the timeline showed roughly two to three days, depending on biological complexity—then rejoined the main timeline. But everything after that rejoining was different. The red futures had collapsed into impossibility. Only blue remained.

The system had fallen into a different attractor basin. Not through force. Through exploiting a moment when the barriers were down.

"ARBITER. Why does this worldline work when the others don't?"

*This worldline compresses generational turnover into a single event. Mortality normally provides correction through death—power resets, wealth redistributes, systems adapt. Immortality disables this mechanism. But temporary death with guaranteed resurrection creates artificial mortality: the corrective function of death without permanent death. The heir operates during this window with the authority that would normally transfer across generations.*

Kaelen stared at the thin blue line.

Centuries of natural correction, compressed into two days. Mortality's function without mortality's permanence.

But the worldline only existed if the decision node occurred.

"Legal Death + Guaranteed Resurrection."

His uncle had the guaranteed resurrection part. The consciousness backup, the clone reserves, the protocols.

He just wasn't planning to die.

Kaelen thought about the Gracchi brothers.

133 BC. Rome was breaking. The Punic Wars had ended, Carthage was destroyed, and Roman soldiers were returning home to find their farms had been seized by aristocrats. The Senate—composed entirely of those same aristocrats—had spent decades consolidating land through legal mechanisms that looked legitimate but were systematically expropriating the small farmers who'd actually won the wars.

There was a law. The Lex Licinia Sextia of 367 BC. It limited individual land holdings to 500 iugera—about 300 acres. The law had been passed to prevent exactly this kind of concentration. But over two centuries, the aristocrats had found workarounds: holding land through family members, through clients, through legal fictions. The law existed. It just wasn't enforced.

Tiberius Gracchus became Tribune of the Plebs in 133 BC. He didn't propose new legislation. He didn't try to reform the system. He just said: "Let's enforce the existing law. Strictly. Literally."

The Senate lost their minds.

Because enforcing the Lex Licinia Sextia meant seizing land from every aristocrat who exceeded the limit. Meant redistributing it to landless veterans. Meant breaking the concentration that had taken two centuries to build.

The aristocrats couldn't argue against it—the law was clear, democratically passed, constitutionally valid. They couldn't claim it was illegal. So they did what aristocrats do when legal mechanisms threaten their position.

They murdered him.

Tiberius Gracchus was beaten to death with wooden clubs by Senators and their supporters. His body was thrown into the Tiber. No trial. No legal process. Just violence when the law stopped serving them.

But here's what mattered to Kaelen: the exploit only worked once.

After Tiberius, the Senate patched the vulnerability. They changed the laws, added exceptions, created new legal mechanisms that made the Lex Licinia Sextia unenforceable. Made sure no one could use that mechanism again.

Tiberius's brother Gaius tried ten years later. Used different tactics, different laws. The Senate murdered him too. Then patched those vulnerabilities.

One shot per exploit. That's what Gödelian vulnerabilities gave you. One execution before the system adapted and closed the gap.

Kaelen had one shot. The window between immortality announcement and legal patch. Once resurrection became public, once legal frameworks recognized that death could be temporary, the Estonian protocols would be amended. The discontinuity would close.

Maybe weeks. Maybe days. But finite. One-time. Unrepeatable.

Kaelen had one shot at this. The window between immortality announcement and legal patch. Maybe weeks. Maybe days. Once resurrection became public, legal frameworks would adapt. The discontinuity would close.

And the trigger—the decision node that created the blue worldline—was death.

Temporary death. Legal death that triggered inheritance. Biological restoration that limited the window.

His uncle had guaranteed resurrection.

Kaelen just had to cause the death.

He sat very still.

The bunker was very quiet.

He stood up. Knocked over his coffee. The mug hit the concrete floor, shattered. Hot coffee spreading across the cold floor, steam rising in the 16°C air.

He didn't move to clean it up.

"No," he said out loud. "That's not—"

He scrolled back through the scenarios. There had to be another way. One of the other 22 blue worldlines. Something that didn't require—

Scenario 12: Global constitutional amendment. Requires 2/3 consensus. The immortals would block it.

Scenario 47: Mandatory resets. Requires enforcement that doesn't exist.

Scenario 134: Revolution. Millions dead.

Scenario 201: Voluntary abdication. Requires immortals choosing to give up power.

He scrolled through all 23. Every single one was either impossible or worse than what he was contemplating.

He sat back down. Coffee was still spreading across the floor. The smell of Ethiopian blend mixing with ozone and cold concrete.

"ARBITER. Confirm: there's no other feasible path?"

*Confirmed. 22 scenarios require conditions that don't exist. One scenario requires creating a specific event.*

"The event being my uncle's death."

*Temporary death. Legal death. The distinction may matter.*

"It's still killing him."

*Technically: inducing temporary cessation of biological function with 99.3% probability of restoration. Colloquially: yes, killing him.*

Kaelen stared at the broken mug. The thin blue worldline on his screen. The decision node labeled "Legal Death + Guaranteed Resurrection."

He'd spent three weeks trying not to think about it. Three weeks trying to convince himself there was another way. 71.4 gigawatt-hours proving there wasn't.

The math was clean. One temporary death versus permanent aristocracy.

But math didn't have to look at his uncle's face. Math didn't have to hug him backstage. Math didn't have to live with it afterward.

He sat in the cold bunker, staring at broken ceramic and spilled coffee, understanding what he had to do.

Not murder. Not really. Temporary death. Induced mortality. Triggering the legal discontinuity that would enable two days of unilateral authority.

Two days to compress centuries of natural correction into systematic restructuring.

Two days to do what mortality would normally do across generations.

He just had to kill his uncle first.

He looked at the compute allocation report:

**TOTAL USAGE: 71.4 gigawatt-hours**  
**RUNTIME: 41 hours**

71.4 gigawatt-hours.

Kaelen pulled up the conversion calculator, made it concrete:

**ENERGY EQUIVALENT:**
- Power 2,400 homes for one month
- Run Manila General Hospital for six months
- Manufacture 47,000 tons of steel
- Desalinate 18 million liters of seawater
- Operate 340 MRI machines for one year

All burned to answer one question: *What happens when the rich live forever?*

The answer: They own everything. Forever.

Was it worth it? 71.4 gigawatt-hours—enough energy to save thousands of lives through direct medical use—spent on modeling a threat that might not even materialize?

He looked at the thin blue worldline. The only path that didn't end in permanent serfdom.

Yeah. It was worth it.

The facility had burned through power for nearly two days straight. No grid connection. No carbon credits. No monthly reports.

Just him, alone underground, discovering that there was only one way to save humanity.

And it required murdering the man he loved.

The bunker's ventilation hummed. The servers cycled. The concrete walls held the cold at 16°C. Somewhere above him, the world was waking up—people making breakfast, going to work, living their mortal lives that would end in 80 years or less.

Down here, there was only the thin blue worldline on his screen and the decision it required.

His uncle would see this on the quarterly audit. Would ask: "What were you researching, Kael?"

What would he say?

*I was modeling what you're about to do to humanity.*

Kaelen closed the results. Encrypted them. Set them to auto-delete in thirty days.

Then he opened a new document and began researching Estonian inheritance law.
