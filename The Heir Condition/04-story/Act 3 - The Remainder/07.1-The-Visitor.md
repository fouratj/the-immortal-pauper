# The Visitor
*Tuesday, April 22, 2050 — Bundang, Gyeonggi Province*

---

The Bundang Silver Community smelled like sujeonggwa and floor wax.

Damon Korr noticed the smell the way his new body noticed everything: as information without history. The old body would have filed it under *institutional, Korean, well-run* and moved on. This body had no files. It received the cinnamon and the wax and the warm mineral breath of the ondol floors as raw data, and somewhere behind his sternum the consciousness that had been reassembled from quantum state vectors forty-eight hours of death ago kept reaching for associations that weren't there. He had been alive for two days. He had been himself for fifty-two years. The two facts were not currently on speaking terms.

Marcus had chartered the flight under a BioGenesis operational account, because Damon's own accounts still believed he was dead. That was the joke he couldn't stop turning over, somewhere over the Pacific: the richest man in human history, borrowing airfare. He had asked for three things. No press. No security detail inside the building. And a facility—any facility, anywhere on Earth—that his own system rated as the best of what the II had built. If he was going to stand inside the wreckage of his life's work, he wanted to start with the part that had worked.

The algorithm returned a list of forty. He picked the one in Korea because the outcome indices were in the 99th percentile, and because it was sixteen hours from San Francisco, and because—though he did not put this in words, and the new body offered no tightening of the chest to make him—Korea was where the development ladder he'd defended in a conference room fourteen months ago had actually been climbed, rung by rung, by people who were still alive to describe the splinters.

The front desk recognized him in stages. The young man at the biometric station looked up, looked down, looked up again with the expression of someone whose brain was returning an authentication paradox of its own. The facility director arrived at a half-run, bowing, apologizing for nothing in particular. Damon asked them not to announce him. He asked—and he could hear how strange it sounded, could see the director's face trying to process it—whether he could simply talk to a resident. Not a tour. Not a briefing on the funding algorithms he had designed and could recite from memory. A conversation.

The director conferred with a care worker—Park Eunji, six years on the morning shift, 87th percentile, though Damon caught himself reading her posture instead of remembering her metrics, which was new. Eunji looked at the most famous man on Earth, twice dead and once resurrected, standing in her lobby in a baseball cap, and made the kind of assessment that no attribution system had ever captured.

"Talk to Yoon Soon-ok," she said. "Room C-14. She'll tell you the truth."

Then, halfway down the hall, she stopped at a small kitchen alcove and picked up a tray with two cups of sujeonggwa on it, and Damon understood that he was being folded into a routine that predated him and would outlast him.

"She has extra today?" he asked.

"It's Tuesday," Eunji said, as if that were an explanation. It was. He just didn't know it yet.

---

The woman in the chair by the window was four foot eleven and a hundred and three pounds including the wool cardigan, and she looked at Damon Korr for a long moment with the frank, unhurried appraisal of someone who had been evaluating men since before his mother was born.

"You died on my television," she said. In English. Her accent was Busan sanded down by seventy years; her grammar was better than most of his board's.

"I did."

"It was very dramatic." She gestured at the bed, the only other place to sit. "Sit. You're too tall. It hurts my neck."

He sat. The bed was firm and close to the floor and the ondol heat came up through the soles of his shoes, and the new body—the body that had never been cold, never been tired, never been anything—registered the warmth as pleasant and could not tell him why it felt like more than that.

Eunji set down the sujeonggwa. Two cups. She placed Soon-ok's on the left side of the bedside table, next to a photograph of a man in a 1956 army uniform, unsmiling, and she did it without looking, the way you do things you have done hundreds of times for reasons you have never been asked to state. Then she left.

Soon-ok watched him watch the cup.

"My husband died on a Tuesday," she said. "Nineteen ninety-nine. Eunji brings extra sujeonggwa on Tuesdays. Nobody told her to. There is no—" she waved a small hand at the ceiling, at the matte-white relay box with its green LED blinking every four seconds, at his entire life's work "—no box for it."

"No," Damon said. "There isn't."

"You built the boxes."

"I built the boxes."

She nodded slowly, sipped her sujeonggwa, and let the silence sit there with the confidence of a woman who had survived worse silences than this one. Damon had negotiated with heads of state. He had held four seconds of global silence in front of ten billion people and used every millisecond. He had no idea what to do with this one.

"So," she said finally. "Did it hurt?"

It took him a moment to understand she meant dying.

"I don't remember it."

"Mm." She considered this. "My husband doesn't remember his either. You have that in common." She said it without cruelty and without softness—a fact, laid on the table between them like a stone from a riverbed. "The television says you are a copy. My granddaughter's colleagues argue about it in the break room. Same memories, different body. They want to know if you are the same person."

"They've been asking me too." He looked at his hands. Unmarked. No callus on the right index finger from thirty years of styluses. No scar on the left palm from the sailing accident in '29. Hands with no past, folded in the lap of a man who was almost nothing but past. "I told a reporter I don't know."

"That was honest. It was on the news for two days. A man that rich, saying *I don't know*." She snorted. "They thought it was a strategy."

"It wasn't."

"I know." She set down her cup. "My little brother went south with me in 1951, on my back. He was two. In 1953 my father came back from the war." She adjusted her cardigan—an automatic gesture, pulling warmth closer in a heated room. "He left one man. He came back a different man. He didn't sleep in the house for a year. He slept in the yard, even in winter. He couldn't be inside walls."

"Was he the same person?"

"Wrong question." She looked at him the way, he imagined, she had looked at seven decades of wrong questions. "He was my father. Same person. Different man. We fed him anyway." A pause. "That is the whole answer. You can spend money to make a better one. There isn't a better one."

The pressure came up in his chest again—the vast oceanic thing that had been arriving in waves since the resurrection chamber, the signal his new body received and did not know how to discharge. The tear ducts were fine. Dr. Chen had tested them. The grief simply arrived at the door of a body that had never learned the way to let it out, and stood there, and waited.

Soon-ok watched his face do whatever it was doing.

"밥 먹었어?" she said.

He knew enough Korean to translate it and not nearly enough to understand it. *Have you eaten?*

"I—" The new body had been fed. Optimized nutrition, administered on a schedule, calibrated to the metabolic requirements of accelerated tissue. "Not really."

"미쳤어," she said, and she was already getting up.

---

He tried to stop her. It went the way it was always going to go.

She produced, from a cabinet he could see and a logic he couldn't, a container of japchae—glass noodles, vegetables cut fine, sesame oil—and a bowl, and she portioned it with the exact economy of someone who had once divided a day's rice into four parts and intended never to forget the arithmetic. She put it in front of him. She said "먹어," which required no translation, and she watched him until he picked up the chopsticks.

Damon Korr had eaten at every restaurant on Earth worth the airfare. He had eaten feijoada in São Paulo cooked by a grandmother whose hands held knowledge no database would ever contain, and he had said, correctly, that the best things resist measurement, and then he had flown home and continued to run the world by measurement, because the measurement was his, and the hands were other people's.

The japchae was the first food this body had ever been given by a person instead of a protocol.

He ate all of it. Somewhere in the middle he stopped being able to see the bowl clearly, and he noted—clinically, distantly, the systems architect reviewing telemetry—that the tear ducts were, in fact, operational after all. Only slightly. A calibration test. The body running its first instance of an ancient program, badly, the way you run anything the first time.

Soon-ok pretended, with great skill, not to see. She refilled his water.

"My granddaughter is a doctor," she said, conversationally, giving him the privacy of her voice to hide inside. "Emergency medicine, Bundang. Three days ago her hospital was full of people whose hearts forgot how to beat because of numbers on their telephones. She worked forty hours. You know what she told me? The machines counted every patient. Every medicine. Every won. And in the middle of the night the nurses were feeding each other kimbap from the machine downstairs, because you cannot intubate a stranger for fourteen hours on an empty stomach, and no one was counting that." She shrugged. "The counting is not bad. I lived without the counting. It was worse. But—"

She stopped. Reached for the words in something deeper than either of her languages.

"When I was seven, in the camp, a woman named Kim Bok-sun divided her rice four ways every day. Her portion, my mother's, mine, my brother's. She had less than we did. Nobody made her. Nobody counted. I asked her why, one time. She said: 혼자 먹으면 다 죽어." She looked at him. "*If I eat alone, we all die.* Your system came fifty years too late to pay Bok-sun. And if it had come in time—" she tapped the table, once, with one finger, a gavel the size of a chopstick "—it still could not have made her share. That is the part you never had. The system doesn't make people share rice. People share rice. The system can only make the room where they do it, or take the room away."

Damon set down the chopsticks. Aligned them on the bowl's rim. His hands wanted a stylus, a tablet, a lever—forty years of reflex reaching for the instrument that turned insight into control. There was no instrument. There was a small warm room, and a photograph of an unsmiling soldier, and a woman who had outlived every system that had ever been imposed on her, feeding the man who had built the latest one.

"Someone broke my system four days ago," he said. "While I was dead. Rebuilt it. In forty-eight hours."

"I saw. The medicine is cheaper. My neighbor's daughter in Manila—" she waved a hand; everyone had a Manila. "And the pension people on the news, crying. Both things."

"Both things," he agreed. "If you could tell him something. The person who did it. What would you say?"

Soon-ok thought about it for a long time. Outside, a bus hissed along the road below. The relay on the ceiling blinked its green pulse, every four seconds, counting what it could.

"When they built this place," she said, "the architects fought for the warm floors. I heard the story from the director; she tells it to everyone. The numbers people said heated floors were expensive. Then the numbers people found out warm floors mean old women don't fall, and they put it in the budget, and now everyone is happy, and they think the numbers found the warmth." She pulled her cardigan tighter. "The numbers didn't find the warmth. Someone who *remembered cold floors* fought for the warmth, and then the numbers caught up. It is always in that order. Always. Tell him that. The counting catches up to the kindness. It never, never arrives first."

She picked up the last yakgwa from a paper bag by her chair—Sungsimdang, he would learn later, the real one, from Daejeon—broke it in half, and put one half in front of him. It was not a metaphor. It was a cookie. It was also not not a metaphor.

"Now eat that," she said. "And then go fix whatever you came here to avoid fixing. You have the face of a man who has a difficult conversation waiting and flew sixteen hours away from it."

Damon laughed. It came out of the new body wrong—too loud, unpracticed, a sound he hadn't made since before the stage. It felt like the first thing this body had done that belonged entirely to him.

"My nephew," he said.

"Mm." If she connected the arithmetic—the death, the heir, the forty-eight hours—her face declined to say so. She had watched systems fail before. She knew that what was left afterward was always the same thing, the only thing, the thing that had walked out of Busan on the backs of seven-year-olds. "Family."

"Family," he said.

"Then eat first." She pushed the yakgwa half a centimeter closer, an old woman moving a piece on a board he was only beginning to see. "Nobody should do family hungry."

---

He left an hour later. At the door of C-14 he stopped, because there was a question the new body had been carrying since the chamber and there would never be a better person to ask.

"Mrs. Yoon. Your brother. Your father. Everyone you lost, everyone who came back different." He hesitated. "Was it worth it—coming back? If you could have—"

"I'm eighty-nine," she said. "Every morning I wake up in a body that forgot how to do a hundred things it used to do. Every morning it is a different body than the one that carried my brother. You think you are the first person to wake up in a new body?" She snorted, and reached for her crossword. "That's just called living, young man. You did it fast. We do it slow. 그래도 살았지."

He didn't ask for a translation. He would look it up on the plane, and then he would sit with it for a long time, somewhere over the Pacific, in the dark, while the world he had built and lost and re-entered went on counting beneath him.

*But still, we lived.*

In the lobby, Eunji was updating the photograph displays—someone's grandchildren, someone's wedding, the rotating archive of everything the funding algorithms made room for and could not produce. Damon stopped next to her.

"The sujeonggwa on Tuesdays," he said. "How did you know?"

Eunji considered the question, and him, and answered the way you answer a slow student you have decided to be patient with.

"I noticed," she said.

He nodded. Forty years, 247 data centers, eleven billion nodes, and the entire time, the load-bearing infrastructure of the world had been running on a protocol of two words, deployed person by person, unlogged, unattributed, since before Korea was Korea.

He walked out into the April afternoon a man who owned nothing, carried nothing, and had, for the first time in either of his lives, been fed.
