# Chapter 6.3: The Gap
*April 20, 2050 — 3:17 AM Eastern*
*Toledo, Ohio*

---

At 3:17 AM, Margaret Kowalski's retirement portfolio crossed a threshold that her financial advisor would later describe as "significant but recoverable" and that her body was already describing, via mechanisms she could have diagrammed on a whiteboard, as cardiac emergency.

She didn't diagram it. She sat at the kitchen table on Drummond Road—the same table where she'd graded forty years of lab reports, taught her daughter to balance chemical equations, and, three years ago, signed the papers that divided Gerald's life into assets and memories and one surprisingly contentious argument about a fishing boat—and watched the numbers fall.

The portfolio app refreshed every thirty seconds. She'd turned off auto-refresh at 1 AM because watching the numbers move was worse than knowing they were moving. Then she'd turned it back on at 1:40 because not watching was worse than watching. Then she'd made tea. Then she'd forgotten the tea. The mug sat next to her phone, cooling at approximately 1.2 degrees Celsius per minute—a number she'd taught her sophomore biology classes as part of the heat transfer unit, demonstrating Newton's law of cooling with coffee mugs and infrared thermometers while fifteen-year-olds stared at their phones and learned nothing about thermodynamics and everything about the futility of enthusiasm.

The portfolio was down 47%.

She'd built it the way responsible people build portfolios: diversified, rebalanced quarterly, pharmaceutical and real estate holdings weighted toward stable dividend producers. She'd read the prospectuses. She'd attended the Fidelity webinars. She'd done the thing the system told you to do if you were a person who followed systems, which she was, which was why she'd taught biology for forty years instead of doing anything more lucrative with a perfectly adequate understanding of organic chemistry.

Pharmaceutical sector: down 58%. Something about patents. Something about commons. The news was using words she recognized individually—"attribution," "cascade," "blockchain settlement"—that combined into sentences she couldn't parse. A man had died on a stage in San Francisco yesterday, or the day before, and now her retirement was evaporating, and the connection between these two events was explained in articles that assumed she understood what a "keystone patent release" was and why it mattered that one had been executed at "blockchain speed."

She didn't understand. She wasn't supposed to understand. She was supposed to be asleep.

Her chest tightened.

She noticed it the way she'd taught her students to notice things: clinically, with the detachment of a person who understood the mechanism. Sympathetic nervous system activation. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis doing exactly what forty years of evolution lectures said it would do—cortisol surging, epinephrine flooding the adrenal medulla, heart rate climbing, blood pressure elevating, the ancient machinery of threat response firing because the body could not distinguish between a predator in the tall grass and a number on a phone screen.

She'd taught this. Had drawn the diagram on the whiteboard every September for four decades, the feedback loop from amygdala to hypothalamus to pituitary to adrenal cortex, the arrows she'd traced so many times the dry-erase marker had worn a groove in the board's surface. *Your body doesn't know the difference between a bear and a math test*, she used to tell them. They'd laugh. She'd meant it. The cortisol was the same cortisol. The cardiac stress was the same cardiac stress. The only difference was that the bear eventually left.

The portfolio refreshed. Down 49%.

The bear was not leaving.

She should call someone. Her daughter, Debbie, was in Portland—three hours behind, so midnight there. Debbie had her own problems. Debbie's husband had left in January and taken the good car and left the Toyota with the transmission that shuddered between second and third in a way that Debbie described as "character" and Margaret described as "a $2,400 repair you can't afford." Calling Debbie at midnight to say *my retirement is disappearing* would accomplish nothing except transferring the cortisol westward.

She could call her sister in Cleveland. Linda would answer. Linda always answered. But Linda would say "I'm sure it'll bounce back" with the confidence of a woman whose retirement was a state pension and a house that was paid off, which was not the same thing as understanding.

She could call Gerald, except Gerald had been dead for three years, and the fishing boat was sold, and the argument about it was the kind of thing that seemed important before death reclassified every argument into the ones that mattered and the ones that were about boats.

The kitchen was quiet. The refrigerator hummed—the compressor cycling in its own feedback loop, maintaining 37°F against the entropy of a house that was slowly, at the molecular level, reaching thermal equilibrium with the Ohio night. She'd taught thermodynamics too. Everything tends toward disorder. The only question is how fast.

She picked up the tea. Cold. Drank it anyway. The tannins were bitter and the temperature was wrong and it was still tea, still the act of lifting a cup, still the gesture of a person doing a normal thing in a kitchen at 3 AM while the financial architecture of her life rearranged itself according to rules she'd never agreed to and couldn't influence.

Her chest tightened again. Harder this time. She pressed her palm flat against her sternum—another gesture she'd taught, though in a different context. *If you feel pressure here*, she'd told the junior varsity soccer players during their annual cardiac awareness assembly, *don't ignore it. The body is talking to you.* She'd said it with the authority of a woman who understood the mechanism. The mechanism was simple. Sustained cortisol elevated blood pressure. Elevated blood pressure increased cardiac workload. Increased cardiac workload, in a seventy-three-year-old heart with the subclinical atherosclerosis that was statistically normal for her age and sex and family history, produced ischemia. Oxygen demand exceeding oxygen supply. The heart eating itself.

She knew this. Could name every step. Could draw the diagram.

The naming did not help.

Four months from now, a cardiac treatment developed by a researcher in Seoul would be available for fourteen dollars—manufactured under commons license, distributed through the same attribution network that was, at this moment, dismantling Margaret's portfolio. The treatment addressed exactly the kind of stress-induced cardiac remodeling that was beginning, tonight, in the muscle fibers of Margaret's left ventricle. It was effective. It was cheap. It was three months away from being manufactured at scale.

Margaret didn't know any of this. Didn't know about the researcher in Seoul or the commons license or the attribution network. Didn't know that her death had been modeled—not as Margaret Kowalski of 414 Drummond Road, Toledo, who had taught biology at Start High School for forty years and whose students still sent her Christmas cards—but as an archetype. A cell in a spreadsheet. One of 2.3 million households. She didn't know that a young man in a bunker had run her scenario two months ago, had seen the four-month gap between portfolio collapse and break-even, had noted the lethality of the interval, and had authorized the cascade anyway.

She sat at her kitchen table. Her chest hurt. The tea was cold. The portfolio refreshed.

Down 51%.

Outside, Toledo was dark and still—the particular stillness of a Midwestern city at 3 AM, sodium lights on Drummond Road casting their orange pall over lawns that Margaret's neighbors mowed on Saturdays, in houses where people were sleeping because they hadn't checked their portfolios, because they didn't know yet, because the market had priced in their futures and their futures hadn't arrived and they were still, for a few more hours, living in the world that was.

In Manila, at that same hour, a woman named Mercedes Bautista was buying medication for six families with cash from a savings pool that no algorithm had ever measured. In Bundang, an eighty-nine-year-old grandmother was asleep in a heated room, unbothered, because she had survived the collapse of systems before and knew that the thing that mattered—the japchae, the sujeonggwa on Tuesdays, the willingness to share rice when there was nothing else—did not run on electricity and could not be switched off by someone in a bunker. The informal economies of care were absorbing the shock the way they always had: invisibly, imperfectly, at a human pace.

Margaret didn't have that. Margaret had done what the system told her to do. Had diversified, rebalanced, attended the webinars. Had trusted the formal architecture completely, because the formal architecture was what respectable people in Toledo trusted. She had no *paluwagan*. No savings pool. No grandmother with a notebook. She had a portfolio app and a kitchen table and a body that was responding to a number on a screen with the same ancient chemistry it would have used for a predator.

She was living in the world that would be. She just couldn't survive the distance.
