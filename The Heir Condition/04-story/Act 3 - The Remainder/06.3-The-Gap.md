# Chapter 6.3: The Gap
*April 20, 2050 — 3:17 AM Eastern*
*Toledo, Ohio*

---

At 3:17 AM, Margaret Kowalski's retirement portfolio crossed a threshold that her financial advisor would later describe as "significant but recoverable" and that her body was already describing, via mechanisms she could have diagrammed on a whiteboard, as cardiac emergency.

She didn't diagram it. She sat at the kitchen table on Drummond Road—the same table where she'd graded forty years of lab reports, taught her daughter to balance chemical equations, and, three years ago, signed the papers that divided Gerald's life into assets and memories and one surprisingly contentious argument about a fishing boat—and watched the numbers fall.

The portfolio app refreshed every thirty seconds. She'd turned off auto-refresh at 1 AM because watching the numbers move was worse than knowing they were moving. Then she'd turned it back on at 1:40 because not watching was worse than watching. Then she'd made tea. Then she'd forgotten the tea. The mug sat next to her phone, cooling. She'd taught Newton's law of cooling with coffee mugs and infrared thermometers. The sophomores had stared at their phones.

The portfolio was down 47%.

She'd built it the way responsible people build portfolios: diversified, rebalanced quarterly, pharmaceutical and real estate holdings weighted toward stable dividend producers. She'd read the prospectuses. She'd attended the Fidelity webinars. She'd done the thing the system told you to do if you were a person who followed systems, which she was, which was why she'd taught biology for forty years instead of doing anything more lucrative with a perfectly adequate understanding of organic chemistry.

Pharmaceutical sector: down 58%. Something about patents. Something about commons. The news was using words she recognized individually—"attribution," "cascade," "blockchain settlement"—that combined into sentences she couldn't parse. A man had died on a stage in San Francisco, and now her retirement was evaporating, and the connection was explained in articles that assumed she understood what a "keystone patent release" was.

She didn't understand. She wasn't supposed to understand. She was supposed to be asleep.

Her chest tightened.

She noticed it clinically. Sympathetic nervous system. Cortisol. Epinephrine. The body could not distinguish a predator in tall grass from a number on a phone. *Your body doesn't know the difference between a bear and a math test*, she used to tell them. They'd laugh. She'd meant it. The only difference was that the bear eventually left.

The portfolio refreshed. Down 49%.

The bear was not leaving.

Debbie was in Portland, midnight there, a shuddering Toyota and a husband who'd left in January. Calling would only ship the cortisol west. Linda in Cleveland would say it would bounce back. Gerald had been dead three years. The fishing boat was sold.

The refrigerator hummed. Entropy. She'd taught that too. Everything tends toward disorder. The only question is how fast.

She drank the cold tea anyway. Still the gesture of a person doing a normal thing in a kitchen at 3 AM while the financial architecture of her life rearranged itself according to rules she'd never agreed to.

The tightness came harder. Palm on sternum. *If you feel pressure here, don't ignore it.* JV soccer, annual assembly. Sustained cortisol. Blood pressure. A seventy-three-year-old heart with the atherosclerosis that was statistically normal. Ischemia. The heart eating itself.

She could name every step. The naming did not help.

A cardiac treatment with a manufacturing cost of fourteen dollars was in a researcher's files in Seoul. It had no place in this kitchen. Margaret did not know the researcher's name. She did not know she had been, to someone, a cell in a spreadsheet.

She sat. The tea was cold. The portfolio refreshed.

Down 51%.

Outside, Toledo was still—sodium light on lawns people would mow on Saturday, houses where people were sleeping because they hadn't checked.

In Manila, a woman named Ate Merced was buying medication for six families with a notebook no algorithm had measured. In Bundang, a hundred-and-six-year-old grandmother was taking an afternoon nap on a heated floor because she had survived the collapse of systems before and knew that sujeonggwa on Tuesdays did not run on a node. Margaret had diversified. Margaret had no paluwagan. Margaret had a portfolio app and a kitchen table and a body answering a number with the chemistry of a hunt.

Her phone was still in reach. The portfolio refreshed again. She tried to close it and touched the wrong part of the screen.
