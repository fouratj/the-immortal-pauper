# The Broadcast
*April 18, 2050 — 00:30 AM Korean Standard Time*
*Seoul National University Hospital, Bundang — Break Room*

---

Seo-jin watched Damon Korr die from the break room on the fourth floor, standing between the vending machine and the microwave, holding a cup of coffee she'd forgotten to drink.

She wasn't supposed to be here. Her shift didn't start for another six hours—she'd come in early to check on Park Jae-won, who was recovering in the cardiac ward, who had nodded at her when she'd looked in and then returned his gaze to the window with the particular concentration of a man relearning how to be alive. The post-catheterization numbers were clean. The community care referral was processing. The system was catching him.

The break room had filled gradually as word spread that the broadcast was starting. Eight nurses, two residents, an anesthesiologist eating kimbap from the convenience store across the street. The wall screen was tuned to KBS, which was carrying the Korr Foundation feed with a simultaneous Korean translation running in subtitles. The translator was struggling—Korr's sentences were long and nested, designed for English-language gravitas, and Korean syntax wanted to put the verb at the end, which meant the translator was always three clauses behind, scrambling to close parentheses that Korr kept opening.

"—the final tyranny. The enemy no genius has ever outrun—"

"드라마 대사 같다," someone muttered. *Sounds like a drama script.* Nervous laughter.

Then the immortality announcement. The room went quiet the way rooms go quiet when the thing you'd half-expected and half-dismissed suddenly becomes real. Seo-jin felt it land in her chest with a weight that was partly professional—*consciousness transfer changes everything about my specialty*—and partly something else. Something she couldn't name yet. The sound of a door opening onto a room she hadn't known existed.

She thought about halmeoni. 그래도 살았지. *But still, I lived.* What happened to that sentence when dying became optional? When "I lived" stopped implying its opposite? When survival was no longer an achievement but a setting?

She thought about Park Jae-won in the cardiac ward. If consciousness transfer worked, his NSTEMI was irrelevant. His isolation was irrelevant. His body was irrelevant—just hardware that could be replaced when it failed. The twelve percentile social connection index would persist across bodies, across decades, across centuries. The loneliness would be immortal too.

Then Korr's hand moved toward his chest.

Seo-jin saw it before anyone else in the room. The micro-gesture—not rhetorical, not dramatic. Involuntary. The particular movement of a man whose left ventricle had just received a signal it didn't understand. She'd seen it a thousand times. She'd seen it four hours ago in Bay 2.

"뭔가 이상해." *Something's wrong.* She said it to no one. The room was still processing the immortality announcement—mouths open, phones out, someone had started crying. Nobody heard her.

Korr swayed. The camera caught it—a fraction of a second of imbalance that the commentators missed but that Seo-jin's body recognized the way her grandmother recognized the bark of the right pine tree. Not a performance. Not a demonstration. A man whose heart was forgetting its rhythm.

"심장이야." *It's cardiac.* Louder now. The anesthesiologist looked up from his kimbap. "저거 봐—ST상승 패턴이야." *Look at that—that's an ST elevation pattern.* She was reading his body through the broadcast feed, through the camera, through the translation of flesh into pixels—reading it the way she read every body that came through her ER, in the language before language, the nunchi that had no attribution score because it existed before measurement.

He fell.

The sound his body made hitting the stage was small on the broadcast—compressed, digitized, stripped of the physical reality that Seo-jin knew from experience: the particular wet thud of a human body in cardiovascular collapse, the specific gravity of a man whose muscles had stopped receiving instructions. The room erupted. The commentators were shouting. The nurses were on their phones. The anesthesiologist set down his kimbap with the careful deliberation of someone who had just realized that the most powerful man in the world was dying on live television and that the kimbap, somehow, didn't matter anymore.

Seo-jin stood still. Watching. Her hands wrapped around the coffee cup—cold now, forgotten—and her mind running the differential diagnosis she couldn't stop running, because that was what her brain did when it saw a body fail: it tried to fix it. From four thousand miles away. Through a screen. The futility of the impulse didn't diminish it.

*Vasoconstriction. Rapid onset. No preceding arrhythmia visible on the broadcast monitors—which means either the monitors aren't showing it or the constriction bypassed the usual electrical precursors. Which means—*

She stopped herself. This was not her patient. This was not her ER. This was the richest man on Earth dying on a stage designed to announce his immortality, and the irony was so vast and so terrible that it felt like something from a Greek tragedy, or a Korean drama, or the particular intersection of hubris and mortality that every civilization eventually produced.

"죽었어?" someone whispered. *Is he dead?*

Nobody answered. On the screen, medical teams were swarming the stage. The commentators had gone silent—the Korean translator had stopped translating because there was nothing left to translate.

Seo-jin's phone buzzed. The hospital's mass notification system: **ALL STAFF ALERT: Potential surge event. Report to assigned stations if on-call.**

She wasn't on-call. She'd come in to check on Park Jae-won.

She went to the ER anyway. Because she was an emergency medicine physician and the world had just broken and there might be patients and she didn't leave when there might be patients. That wasn't attribution talking. That wasn't the system.

That was 그래도—*but still*—the conjunction that refused to let the sentence end.
