Candidate 2: A physician-researcher in Seoul, South Korea.
South Korea is the other classic early-adopter story — a country that went from post-war devastation to technological powerhouse in a single lifetime. The development ladder worked there, which gives it direct relevance to Damon's Coalbrookdale argument.
This person is a clinician and researcher. They treat patients and develop treatments. They live at the intersection of care work and innovation — the same intersection Rosa occupies, but from the other end. Where Rosa's care work is undervalued by the system, this person's is correctly valued. They see the system working. They also see, because they're a doctor, what it costs when it doesn't.
Their kitchen: a small apartment in Mapo-gu, near the university hospital. The smell of doenjang-jjigae simmering. The hum of Seoul at night — that particular density of a city that never quite sleeps. Medical journals stacked on the dining table. The residue of a life lived between the lab and the ward.
Their problem: they're developing a treatment — maybe related to the GlucoSync technology, or something adjacent — and they know the patent licensing will make it unaffordable for most of the world. They've been to conferences where colleagues from Manila and Lagos and Dhaka have told them what the licensing costs do. They carry that knowledge the way a doctor carries the knowledge of a disease they can diagnose but not cure. It's not guilt exactly. It's the particular ache of someone whose work is good but whose system is insufficient.
Maybe they have a personal connection too. A grandmother who survived the Korean War. A family history of poverty within living memory. They're only one or two generations removed from being Rosa. They know this. It informs everything.
During the cascade: the hospital's funding model destabilizes. Research grants freeze. For a day, they think their lab might close. They stay up all night monitoring their patients, making sure the disruption doesn't kill anyone. The fear is real — not for themselves but for their patients, who depend on infrastructure that's suddenly uncertain.
Then: the patents go to commons. Their treatment — the one they knew was too expensive — is suddenly manufacturable at cost. Their research becomes more valuable, not less, because it's no longer locked behind licensing. The hospital restructures as a non-profit and their salary stays the same but their work reaches ten times more people. They walk through the ward and realize: every patient here can now afford what they're receiving.
The appeal: The mirror to Rosa. Both are care workers. Both are inside the system's measurement of human value. One is correctly measured, one isn't. The symmetry is precise without being mechanical. And the personal history — grandmother who survived the war, family that climbed the development ladder — gives them a perspective on Damon's Coalbrookdale argument that isn't abstract. They are Derek's grandchild. They know the cost was real. And they know the benefit was real too.
The concern: South Korea doesn't have existing thematic presence in the manuscript the way Estonia does. You'd be introducing a new geography.
