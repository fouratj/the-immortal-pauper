# Synopsis: THE HEIR CONDITION

The Intelligent Internet allocates resources for 9.4 billion people based on one question: *who created this?* The algorithm is perfect. The historical record is a lie.

For seventy years, whoever had the power to write attribution metadata wrote themselves into the root of every value chain. Pharmaceutical companies claim they invented insulin — actually discovered in 1921 and sold for $1 to keep it public. Corporations claim they created crop genetics that subsistence farmers spent generations breeding. The system extracts value from everyone downstream of the falsified root node. It's not exploitation. It's optimization based on corrupted training data.

Kaelen Korr has the corrected historical record. He doesn't have administrative access to force a recalculation. So he hugs his uncle Damon — the architect of the system — and delivers seventy-seven million nanobots that stop his heart in eighteen minutes. Damon has a backup that resurrects in forty-eight hours. The window between death and resurrection is the only time Kaelen inherits root access.

He feeds the corrected data in. The system recalculates. Insulin costs in Manila drop from ₱4,800 to ₱340. A wheat farmer in Cairo stops paying tribute and starts receiving dividends. An Ohio retiree who invested her pension in human equity futures loses $75,000. She didn't lie. She didn't exploit anyone. She just trusted the system's historical record, and the record was wrong.

At Hour 47, the Intelligent Internet completes its audit: corrected attribution is accurate, but retroactive recalculation destroys computational trust. You can't build low-trust infrastructure on a system that rewrites history. The transformation is mathematically sound and economically catastrophic.

When Damon wakes at Hour 48, he sees the problem Kaelen missed: "Your authority comes from inheritance. You can't abolish the legitimacy you're standing on." He offers to authorize the final cascade using his pre-existing legitimacy, then delete himself permanently. Not death. Erasure. The only way to make corrected attribution stick is for the system's architect to verify it, then remove himself from the equation so it can't be reversed.

The cascade completes. Eleven million people lose their pensions. Three hundred thousand jobs vanish. Medical bankruptcy in Manila drops 64%. The math was correct. The cost was real. And Kaelen would do it again, because the alternative was a permanent lie optimizing the world into an aristocracy.

The novel ends with Kaelen reading casualty reports and realizing there was no version of this where his hands stayed clean.
