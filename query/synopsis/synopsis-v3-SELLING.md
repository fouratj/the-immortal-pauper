# Synopsis: THE HEIR CONDITION

Kaelen Korr has seventy-two hours to rewrite history before his uncle wakes up.

The Intelligent Internet governs resource allocation for 9.4 billion people based on attribution metadata — who created what, who owns what, who contributed to the value chain. The algorithm works perfectly. The problem is the data. For seventy years, the people with power to define "contribution" have been writing themselves into the root nodes of every dependency graph. Patent holders claim they "invented" insulin. Corporations claim they "created" crop genetics subsistence farmers spent generations breeding. The system allocates value downstream from whoever controls the attribution, and the attribution has been systematically falsified.

Kaelen has the corrected data — census records, lab notebooks, shipping manifests, oral histories. Three years of work reconstructing what was deliberately erased. What he doesn't have is administrative access to force a global recalculation. So he hugs his uncle in front of 10.3 billion witnesses and delivers seventy-seven million medical nanobots reprogrammed to stop Damon's heart. Damon has a digital backup scheduled to resurrect in forty-eight hours. The window between death and resurrection is the only time Kaelen inherits administrative control.

He feeds the corrected attribution into the system. The Intelligent Internet recalculates. Insulin traced back to Banting, who sold the patent for $1 — medication costs in Manila drop from ₱4,800 to ₱340. Smartphone supply chains traced back to coltan miners in the DRC — wages recalculate. Wheat genetics traced to Cairo farmers — tribute payments reverse into dividends. The system is doing exactly what it was designed to do: optimize based on accurate data.

But the recalculation is destabilizing. An Ohio retiree who invested her pension in human equity futures loses $75,000. She didn't commit fraud. She played by the rules. The rules just changed retroactively. Multiply her by eleven million. The math works. The history is accurate. The cost is catastrophic.

At Hour 47, the Intelligent Internet delivers its audit: the corrected attribution is sound, but the implementation destroys the system's computational legitimacy. You can't build low-trust economic infrastructure on a platform that rewrites history. Rational actors defect. Markets collapse. You can have justice or you can have stability, but the state space that gives you both doesn't exist — unless someone with *pre-existing* legitimacy authorizes the transformation.

When Damon wakes up at Hour 48, he reads the audit report and sees the incompleteness theorem Kaelen missed: "Your authority comes from inheritance. You're using the master's tools to dismantle the master's house." Damon offers to complete the cascade using his own legitimacy, then delete himself permanently — no backup, no resurrection. The only way to make corrected attribution irreversible is for the architect to authorize it, then remove himself from the equation.

Kaelen has to choose: let his uncle complete a mathematically correct transformation that will bankrupt eleven million people, or stop him and live with a world where the corrected data was accurate but unimplementable. There's no utilitarian calculus that makes one choice obviously right. Just two kinds of guilt.

The novel ends with the cascade complete and Kaelen reading casualty reports. The math was correct. The history was accurate. The cost was real. And he'd do it again, because the alternative was a world where the lie was permanent.

**THE HEIR CONDITION** (65,000 words) — a science fiction novel about algorithmic governance, falsified attribution data, and what it costs to make a system count truthfully when the truth arrives seventy years late.
