# Synopsis: THE HEIR CONDITION

## The System

The Intelligent Internet governs resource allocation for 9.4 billion people. It's not artificial general intelligence — it's something more dangerous: a perfectly functioning optimization engine running on corrupted data.

Every transaction, every patent claim, every equity contract feeds metadata into the system: *who created this, who owns this, who contributed to this*. The algorithm allocates value based on attribution. The problem isn't the algorithm. The problem is that for seventy years, the people with enough power to define "contribution" have been writing their own attribution into the historical record.

Damon Korr built this system. He also built something else: a distributed backup of his consciousness, scheduled to resurrect in forty-eight hours if his body dies. Digital immortality with a time delay — a safety mechanism against assassination. Once he comes back, the math says the system becomes permanent. Kaelen has run every scenario. In 90.7% of possible futures where Damon achieves immortality, the wealth concentration curve never reverses. Not metaphorical permanence. Actual mathematical lock-in.

The only window is the forty-eight hours between death and resurrection.

## The Attribution Problem

Kaelen doesn't hack the Intelligent Internet. He doesn't shut it down. He does something more fundamental: he corrects the training data.

The system allocates value based on contribution graphs — vast networks of dependencies tracing who built what on top of what. Patent pools. Supply chains. Genetic lineages of crops. Every manufactured object is a tree of attribution, and whoever controls the root nodes extracts value from everything downstream.

For seventy years, those root nodes have been systematically misattributed.

Example: Insulin. Seventeen patents between the molecule and a diabetic child in Manila. The attribution graph says the patent holders "invented" insulin. The historical record says Frederick Banting discovered it in 1921 and refused to profit from it, selling the patent to the University of Toronto for $1 so it would remain accessible. The Intelligent Internet allocates value based on the graph it can see, not the history it was never trained on.

Kaelen has the historical data. Census records. Lab notebooks. Shipping manifests. Oral histories. Three years of work reconstructing seventy years of deliberately erased attribution. He has the data. What he doesn't have is the authority to force the system to recalculate.

Until his uncle dies, and he becomes the sole administrator.

## The Execution

Kaelen hugs Damon in front of 10.3 billion witnesses and delivers seventy-seven million medical nanobots reprogrammed to induce cardiac arrest. Eighteen minutes later, Damon collapses on stage. Forty-eight hours starts counting down.

Kaelen has administrative access. He feeds the corrected attribution data into the Intelligent Internet and forces a global recalculation.

The system audits seventy years of metadata. It traces insulin back to Banting and reassigns the patent pool to public domain. Rosa Santos's daughter's medication drops from ₱4,800 to ₱340 per month. It traces smartphone supply chains back to the women mining coltan in the Democratic Republic of Congo and reassigns extraction fees. It traces wheat genetics to subsistence farmers in Cairo whose ancestors bred the strains that feed half of Egypt, and reverses the tribute payments into dividends.

The Intelligent Internet is doing exactly what it was designed to do: optimize resource allocation based on accurate attribution data. Kaelen didn't break the system. He fixed its inputs.

But the outputs are destabilizing.

A 73-year-old retiree in Ohio invested her pension in human equity futures — contracts that pay dividends based on children's lifetime earnings. The recalculation voids those contracts. She loses $75,000. She didn't commit fraud. She didn't exploit anyone. She invested according to the rules she was given, and now the rules have changed retroactively.

Multiply her by eleven million.

## The Incompleteness Theorem

At Hour 47, the Intelligent Internet delivers its final audit report. The recalculation is mathematically sound. The corrected attribution is historically accurate. And the implementation is impossible.

Here's why: the system's legitimacy depends on *continuity*. Contracts, property rights, pension funds — all of these require that yesterday's rules still apply today. A system that retroactively rewrites attribution at scale is indistinguishable from arbitrary confiscation. Trust collapses. Not moral trust — *computational* trust. The low-trust economic topology that allows strangers to transact across borders requires cryptographic guarantees that the system won't rewrite history.

Kaelen is trying to use a system built on immutability to rewrite the past. It's not a technical problem. It's a *logical* one. The system can execute the transformation, but it can't guarantee its own legitimacy afterward. Every agent operating under the new rules has to accept that the system might change the rules again. Rational actors defect. Markets collapse.

The math is unambiguous: you can have corrected attribution or you can have economic stability, but the state space that gives you both doesn't exist.

Unless someone with *pre-existing* legitimacy authorizes it.

## The Inheritance

Damon wakes up at Hour 48 in a body that's never been used. His neural patterns are identical to the moment of death, but the world has moved on without him. The nephew who killed him has spent two days dismantling his empire, and the system is holding at a catastrophic equilibrium — correct but unstable.

The confrontation is not what Kaelen expects.

Damon doesn't rage. He reads the audit report. He looks at the corrected attribution data — the census records, the lab notebooks, the oral histories. And he sees the incompleteness theorem Kaelen missed.

"You thought killing me would give you the authority to fix this. But your authority comes from *inheritance*. Every authorization you make is backed by the same property rights you're trying to abolish. You're not outside the system. You're using the master's tools to dismantle the master's house, and the tools don't work for that."

Damon offers a solution: he authorizes the final cascade himself, then deletes his own backup permanently. Not death — *deletion*. No resurrection. No recovery. The only way to make the transformation irreversible is for the system's architect to authorize it using his pre-existing legitimacy, and then remove himself from the equation.

It's not redemption. It's logic. Damon isn't sacrificing himself. He's completing a proof.

But there's a cost. The cascade executes. Eleven million retirees lose their pensions. Three hundred thousand jobs in the patent licensing industry vanish. Supply chains break. The corrected system is more *just*, but justice at this scale has casualties measured in millions.

Kaelen has to decide: does he let his uncle complete the transformation, knowing people will suffer? Or does he stop him, and live with a world where the corrected attribution was technically accurate but practically unimplementable?

There's no clean answer. That's the point. The math doesn't offer a utilitarian calculus that makes one choice obviously correct. It offers two kinds of guilt, and you have to pick one.

## The Outcome

The cascade completes. The Intelligent Internet recalculates global value allocation based on corrected historical attribution. Rosa's daughter gets insulin. The retiree in Ohio loses her pension. Both of these are outputs of the same recalculation.

The world doesn't become a utopia. It becomes a system that *counts differently*. Subsistence farmers in Cairo get dividends instead of tribute bills. Miners in the Congo get wages that reflect their position in the supply chain. Medical bankruptcy in Manila drops by 64% because pharmaceuticals are allocated based on need rather than ability to pay.

But the transition has a body count. And Kaelen inherits it.

The novel ends with Kaelen alone in the bunker, reading through casualty reports — the eleven million pension losses, the supply chain collapses, the workers displaced by the recalculation. The math was correct. The history was accurate. The cost was real.

He authorized it. And he'd do it again.

Because the alternative was a world where the lie was permanent.

**THE HEIR CONDITION** is 65,000 words. It's a science fiction novel about algorithmic governance, contested attribution, and what it costs to make a system count truthfully when the truth is seventy years late.
