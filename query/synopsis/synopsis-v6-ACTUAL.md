# Synopsis: THE HEIR CONDITION

In 2050, the Intelligent Internet governs resource allocation for 9.4 billion people. No politicians. No corruption. Constitutional rights encoded in cryptographic substrate. Resource distribution based on verifiable contribution chains — if you created something, the system pays you. If you built on someone else's work, value splits according to the dependency graph. It's not communism, not capitalism. It's math. And in democratic early adopters like Estonia, Singapore, and Switzerland, it works: 25-40% GDP growth, 87-92% approval ratings, care workers earning what financial engineers earn because the Tallinn Coefficients measure opportunity cost elimination, not market price. Damon Korr built this. Nine billion people trust it because it's incorruptible by design.

Except the training data is poison. Attribution metadata determines who gets paid — and for twenty years, people with root access have been rewriting history. They claimed they "invented" insulin (actually discovered in 1921 by Frederick Banting, who sold the patent for $1). They claimed they "created" smartphone technology (built on DARPA-funded public research). They claimed they "bred" crop genetics that subsistence farmers spent generations developing. In Estonia, the corrected attribution works because democratic oversight verified the data. In Manila, Cairo, Lagos — the late adopters forced into the system by economic coercion — existing power structures encoded themselves as foundational contributors. The algorithm that creates abundance in democracies enables extraction in autocracies. Same math. Different inputs.

Kaelen Korr has forty-eight hours between his uncle's death and digital resurrection to feed corrected attribution data into the system and force a global recalculation. He hugs Damon in front of 10.3 billion witnesses and delivers seventy-seven million nanobots. Eighteen minutes later, Damon collapses on stage. The constitutional system executes exactly as designed: it reallocates value based on verified contribution. Insulin costs in Manila drop from ₱4,800 to ₱340. A wheat farmer in Cairo stops paying tribute on genetics his grandfather bred. Patent thickets dissolve. Housing speculation ends. And eleven million people who invested their pensions in human equity futures — legal, constitutional contracts under the falsified attribution regime — lose everything. They didn't lie. They didn't exploit anyone. They trusted the system's historical record. The record was wrong.

The novel ends with the corrected system working exactly as originally designed. It's just that restoring truth twenty years late has casualties measured in millions. Kaelen reads the reports and realizes there was no version of this where his hands stayed clean. The alternative was a permanent lie optimizing the world into an aristocracy. He made his choice. And he'd make it again.
