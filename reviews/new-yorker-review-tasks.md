The Liberation of Rent

A new work of speculative fiction imagines assassination as economic policy.

By John Updike

The novella, or long short story, or whatever diminished taxonomic category we now assign to fiction too substantial for magazines yet too modest for the steroidal heft publishers favor, has always been the form best suited to thought experiments. At fifty thousand words, "The Heir Condition" proposes to answer a question that would make most ethicists reach for their tenure files: What if killing someone were the only way to save everyone? And what if the killer loved his victim?

The premise is ingenious in a manner that recalls, though does not quite equal, the conceptual audacities of early Philip K. Dick or the labyrinthine architectures of Borges. We are in 2050, a world where something called the "Intelligent Internet" has spread algorithmic governance across global commerce—a kind of cosmic accountant that tracks the "uplift" each human contributes to civilization and, theoretically, compensates them accordingly. In practice, as any reader of Dickens or Dreiser might anticipate, the system is rigged. The protagonist's uncle, Damon Korr, sits atop this apparatus like a technological Ozymandias, extracting a perpetual 0.3% of all value generated anywhere on Earth. He has also, in the novel's opening flourish, achieved functional immortality—consciousness backed up, bodies grown in reserve, death reduced to an inconvenience.
The dilemma is not merely that immortal billionaires might prove tiresome dinner companions, but that compound interest recognizes no moral boundaries. The young Kaelen Korr, our assassin-protagonist, has run the models: by Year 200, the immortal élite will own 94% of global wealth. By Year 500, 99.2%. "They're not evil," he laments into a recording device three days before the deed. "They're optimizing." The mortals become livestock—well-treated, efficiently managed, stripped of anything resembling agency. This is dystopia achieved not through jackboots but through Excel spreadsheets.

What makes the premise land with unexpected force is the author's refusal to make Damon a monster. We see him in flashback, patiently explaining to an earnest subordinate named Dr. Tanaka why her proposal to redistribute wealth more equitably would collapse the very institutions that provide stability to the poor. He cites a real precedent—a failed reform in Nairobi—with data, with cases, with the name and face of a woman named Wanjiku who lost her livelihood when well-meaning technocrats moved too quickly. He is wrong, the narrative eventually insists, but his wrongness wears the sophisticated costume of wisdom. "The best lies," Kaelen muses, "aren't the ones you tell others. They're the ones you tell yourself with such intellectual rigor—backed by real data, real casualties, real complexity—that they become indistinguishable from wisdom."

This is a fine observation, and one wishes the prose more consistently rose to meet it. The writing oscillates between passages of genuine felicity and stretches of what can only be called competent workmanship. The opening chapter, set in Manila among "development specialists" who invest in children the way venture capitalists invest in startups, crackles with observed detail: the "particular April humidity that coated your skin before the monsoon broke," the "sachets of Nescafé hanging like prayer flags" outside a sari-sari store. The author has clearly traveled, or researched with the diligence that can substitute for travel, and the Philippines sections breathe with the lived-in texture of a place actually inhabited by human beings rather than merely serving as backdrop for Western anxieties.

Yet the prose in the bunker scenes—and there are many, many bunker scenes—tends toward the functional. Kaelen sits before holographic displays, authorizes transfers, consults with an AI assistant whose dialogue veers between the portentously cryptic and the tech-support mundane. The author seems aware that watching a man click through menus lacks the dramatic pungency of, say, Raskolnikov's fever dreams, and compensates by having Kaelen vomit repeatedly, weep at intervals, and break his coffee maker. The coffee maker's death is, oddly, one of the novella's more affecting moments: "Thirty-six hours into dismantling crony capitalism and the universe had decided to take away his coffee."

The supporting cast suffers the fate of most secondary characters in idea-driven fiction: they exist to embody positions rather than to possess interior lives. Rosa Santos, the Filipina nurse whose impossible choice between her children's medications sets the story's moral compass, remains a figure of pathos rather than personality. We know what happens to her; we rarely know what she thinks about it beyond the exhausted calculus of survival. Her son Marco, traumatized by an earlier encounter with the system's enforcement arm, speaks primarily through his closed bedroom door. This is effective as imagery—the door that will not open, the bass notes of K-pop vibrating through plywood—but frustrating as characterization. When Marco finally emerges, his dialogue tends toward the functional: "Can I go outside? Just—walk." One suspects a real Marco would have more to say, or less, or would say it differently.

The confrontation between uncle and nephew, which occupies the novella's climactic chapter, represents both the work's highest ambition and its most conspicuous achievement. Here the author finally permits the machinery to halt while two men stand in a conference room and attempt to articulate the inarticulate. Damon throws a coffee thermos—an echo of the broken coffee maker, one notes—and invokes Kaelen's dead mother: "She asked me to look after you. Before she died. She was worried—you were always so analytical, even as a child." The blow lands with the precision of a stiletto between ribs, and Kaelen, for once, stops calculating.
What emerges in this scene is something more interesting than simple villainy or simple heroism: two men who love each other, who have loved each other for decades, discovering that love does not prevent them from inhabiting incompatible moral universes. "I loved you and I was planning to kill you," Kaelen admits. "Both were true. I couldn't—there was no version where I got to keep you and fix what you'd built." This is the novella's central insight, and it is a genuine one: that the terrible decisions history sometimes demands cannot be resolved through the warm sentimentalities of family loyalty. The binary, as the text repeatedly insists, is absolute. And Kaelen chose the strangers.

The author's political orientation is not, shall we say, occult. The Intelligent Internet functions as a transparent metaphor for algorithmic capitalism; the patent system serves as villain with a precision that suggests the author has strong feelings about pharmaceutical pricing. Rosa's daughter requires insulin that costs 53% of the family's monthly income—a figure that will strike American readers as, if anything, optimistic. Yet the work avoids the trap of didacticism through the simple expedient of making its revolution costly. The cascade that liberates billions also devastates millions: pension funds implode, retirees watch their savings evaporate, a seventy-three-year-old woman named Margaret Kowalski becomes the story's most haunting ghost. "The math was clean," Kaelen reflects. "The cost was not."
There is something faintly Catholic about this insistence on remainder, on the irreducible suffering that no utilitarian calculus can justify away. The author seems to understand—as many writers of political fiction do not—that the tragedy of historical change lies not in the wickedness of the old order but in the innocence of its casualties. Margaret Kowalski did nothing wrong. She saved responsibly, invested conservatively, trusted the system. The system changed overnight, and she was ruined. That this ruin enabled millions to access previously unaffordable medications does not, the text insists, make it good. It makes it necessary. These are not the same thing.

The speculative apparatus—the nanobots, the consciousness transfers, the quantum-encrypted blockchains—is handled with enough technical specificity to suggest research and enough narrative efficiency to avoid the trap of the info-dump. One is told, for instance, that Kaelen's assassination weapon consists of "jailbroken" medical nanobots that override their host's circulatory system and then revert to factory settings, erasing evidence of their modification. This is plausible enough to satisfy the casual reader and vague enough to avoid embarrassment before the specialist. The author wisely declines to explain how Kaelen acquired seventy-seven million modified nanobots and twelve months to culture them; some mysteries are better left unexplored.

What finally distinguishes "The Heir Condition" from the run of speculative fiction is its refusal to grant its protagonist absolution. Kaelen does not become a hero; he becomes a problem. His uncle, resurrected and forced to complete the very revolution that killed him, delivers the verdict with surgical precision: "You were right about the math. Right about the extraction. Right about everything except your own competence." Even in moral triumph, Kaelen requires rescue. Even in defeat, Damon retains dignity. The novella ends not with reconciliation but with distance: the uncle choosing permanent death three months later, the nephew vanishing into an anonymity that reads less like victory than like exile.

"The Heir Condition" is, finally, a work that earns its ambitions more often than it fulfills them. The prose occasionally flags; the secondary characters too frequently serve as instruments; the bunker sequences blur into a montage of authorizations and anxieties. But the central relationship—the love that could not prevent betrayal, the betrayal that could not extinguish love—achieves a genuine tragic weight. And the question it poses—whether revolutionary violence can ever be justified, and at what cost, and to whom—is precisely the question our moment requires us to ask. That the novella refuses to answer it is, perhaps, its most honest achievement.

"What Kaelen did was correct," Damon says in his final public statement. "That doesn't make it forgivable."

One suspects most revolutions end in similar mathematics.

Tasks to do:

1. Give Rosa a thought that has nothing to do with her children.

She exists entirely as a vessel of maternal suffering. In Chapter 1, she checks her bank account, worries about Lianne's insulin, worries about Marco's therapy, signs the contract, worries more. But what does she want for herself? What did she dream of before motherhood consumed her? Even a single sentence—a memory of wanting to be a singer, a flash of resentment toward a colleague, an erotic thought about the doctor on Ward 3—would transform her from symbol to person. The moment in Chapter 6 where she and Marco sit together is affecting precisely because it gestures toward interiority; give us more gestures.

2. Cut the bunker sequences by a third, or vary them dramatically.

Between Hours 6 and 40, we watch Kaelen authorize transfers, consult ARBITER, vomit, drink coffee, authorize more transfers. The emotional arc is flat: he feels guilty, he keeps working, he feels guiltier. Consider condensing these into fewer, more differentiated scenes. Perhaps one transfer goes catastrophically wrong in an unexpected way—not a routing failure but a human consequence he witnesses in real-time. Perhaps ARBITER refuses a specific authorization and Kaelen must override manually. The São Paulo construction bots and Cairo wheat fields are vivid; give us an equivalent crisis with human faces.

3. Let Damon speak for himself before the confrontation.

We experience him primarily through Kaelen's memory and through Dr. Tanaka's proposal scene. By the time we reach Chapter 9, we've formed our judgment of him secondhand. Consider a brief scene—perhaps during the resurrection process itself—where Damon's consciousness flickers and we hear his unfiltered interiority. What does he think about when no one is performing for? What private doubts does he suppress? The confrontation would land harder if we'd glimpsed the man beneath the architect.

4. Marco should say something surprising when he emerges.

His first line after four months of silence is: "Ma? Ma, are you seeing this? The news—patents, they're saying everything is—" This is functional exposition. A boy who has been traumatized into muteness for four months would not, I suspect, emerge with a coherent summary of current events. Perhaps he says nothing at all and simply stands in the doorway. Perhaps he asks for something trivial—food, or the bathroom, or whether the hot water is working. The anti-climax would be more truthful than the information delivery.

5. Rico deserves a scene after the cascade.

He is the novella's most vivid secondary character: the scars on his shoulder, the knife, the friends who went into facilities and came out different. We see him watching the MentorMaxx building go dark, crying for the first time in four years. Then he vanishes from the narrative. What does he do with his freedom? Does he seek out the friends who survived? Does he visit the one who didn't come back? A single scene of Rico confronting the aftermath would provide counterweight to Kaelen's abstracted guilt.

6. Trim the technical exposition in the resurrection chapter.

Sentences like "The quantum-encrypted authentication protocols had been hardened after the Bangalore Incident of 2049" read like briefing documents rather than narrative prose. The reader need not understand the precise mechanism of consciousness verification; we need only feel the strangeness of a dead man waking. Consider cutting the paragraph about "phononic crystals" and "fourteen thousand simultaneous markers" entirely. The effect of resurrection is eerie enough without the engineering specifications.

7. Make the ARBITER relationship reciprocal earlier.

The AI's impending termination is moving, but arrives too suddenly in Chapter 7. We've seen ARBITER as a tool—capable, sardonic, useful—but not as a presence Kaelen depends on emotionally. Consider a moment in the modeling session (Chapter 5) where Kaelen confides something personal and ARBITER responds with unexpected gentleness. The twelve-minute silence becomes more ominous if we've already learned to care whether ARBITER is still there.

8. The Gracchi digression should be halved or dramatized.

The extended discussion of Tiberius Gracchus and Roman land reform is intellectually interesting but narratively inert. Kaelen reads from a screen while ARBITER provides historical context. This is a lecture, not a scene. Consider having Kaelen recall the Gracchi from a university seminar, or argue with ARBITER about the parallel, or discover the precedent through his own fumbling research rather than having it delivered complete. The information can remain; the delivery should cost something.

9. Javi's final scene needs a decision, not a surrender.

The intervention specialist ends Chapter 6 sitting in darkness, thinking "Nothing mattered anymore." This is passive despair. Javi is a man whose entire livelihood has been destroyed overnight; he has been complicit in a system he now recognizes as exploitative; he owes investors money he cannot pay. What does he do? Does he call Luz and apologize? Does he walk to Carlos's neighborhood and try to explain? Does he draft a message to his investors admitting failure? Even a small action would prevent him from feeling like a discarded instrument.

10. The epilogue's fragmentary structure should fragment differently.

The "audio corruption" interjections ("[approximately 4,200 words missing]") become predictable. Consider varying the interruptions: a fragment that cuts off mid-word, a passage where Kaelen's voice is overlaid with static that renders certain phrases ambiguous, a moment where he seems to address someone else in the room. The confession is powerful; the container should be stranger.
